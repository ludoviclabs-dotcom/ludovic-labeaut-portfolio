Iran–United States: anatomy of a war with no end state

Seven months after the strikes of 28 February, there is neither military victory nor agreement. Why the conflict has settled into a stalemate, who is wearing down fastest, and what could tip it before the end of the year.

By Ludovic Labeaut10 min readData as of 26 September 2026

Key points

  1. US objectives have drifted: the regime and the nuclear programme in February, the Strait of Hormuz in July, then “a deal or annihilation” in September. With no criterion for victory, there is no way out.
  2. The heart of the conflict is no longer the nuclear programme but sovereignty over Hormuz. For Washington, the stakes are logistical; for Tehran, they are existential.
  3. This is an industrial war of attrition. According to CSIS, roughly two-thirds of the US Patriot stockpile had been used up by July. An interceptor costs about $4m, a Shahed drone about $50,000.
  4. Two clocks are pitted against each other: the 3 November midterm elections for Donald Trump, economic collapse for Tehran (the rial at 2.25 million to the dollar, inflation close to 90%).

01Seven months, four phases

The conflict is part of a sequence that began in 2025. In June, the “Twelve-Day War” ended with US strikes on Fordo, Natanz and Isfahan (Operation “Midnight Hammer”, 22 June)1. At the end of September, France, Germany and the United Kingdom (the E3) secured the reinstatement of UN sanctions through the snapback mechanism2. The following winter, the collapse of the rial sparked an uprising, which was bloodily suppressed.

On 28 February 2026, US-Israeli strikes (Operations “Epic Fury” and “Roaring Lion”) opened the war. Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was killed; his son Mojtaba succeeded him on 8 March3. Since then, the conflict has gone through four phases (Figure 1):

  • Open war (40 days). Iran retaliated against Israel and against bases and infrastructure across the Gulf.
  • The armed ceasefire. It was agreed on 8 April through Pakistani mediation. The Islamabad talks failed, and on 13 April Washington imposed a naval blockade of Iranian ports4.
  • The memorandum of understanding (MoU) truce. Signed on 17 and 18 June, it lifted the blockade but deferred the nuclear question5.
  • The resumption. Strikes on ships on 6 and 7 July reignited the conflict, and the blockade was reimposed on the 14th6. Then came the Treasury's “Economic D-Day” on 24 August7, the tit-for-tat clashes of late August and early September (Iranian tankers sunk, missiles fired towards Jordan)8, and the IAEA's referral to the Security Council on 9 September9.

The latest sequence is diplomatic in appearance only. On 22 September, three hours of indirect talks in New York, with Qatar and Pakistan acting as intermediaries, marked the first official contact since June10. On the 25th, Tehran offered to reopen Hormuz within seven days in exchange for the lifting of the blockade, a ceasefire extending to Lebanon and the unfreezing of $12bn in assets. According to the Wall Street Journal, Donald Trump has rejected the plan and is considering resuming the bombing after the midterm elections11.

02Why there is no “end state”

In military doctrine, the “desired end state” is the political situation in which an operation can be considered to have achieved its aim. That is precisely what is missing here. There are five reasons for this.

1. Shifting objectives. In February, Washington was targeting the regime, the nuclear programme, the missiles and Tehran's regional proxies all at once. By July, the objective had narrowed to Hormuz. In September, at the UN rostrum, it became “a deal or annihilation”12. At a CFR roundtable on 8 September, one participant summed up the ambiguity: is this about the strait, the nuclear programme or the regime13? In Lawrence Freedman's view, by trying to avoid a quagmire, America has found a stalemate14.

2. An indivisible issue. For Washington, Hormuz is a problem of flows. For Tehran, it is an attribute of sovereignty, and Vali Nasr (CSIS) observes that Iran now regards its control of the strait as “irreversible”15. The June MoU left this question in deliberate ambiguity: its Article 5 refers to a dialogue between Iran and Oman on the administration of the passage12. The nuclear issue, meanwhile, has slipped into the background even though uncertainty has never been greater. The IAEA has verified nothing in Iran since 28 February, and the last stockpile it checked, in June 2025, stood at 440.9 kg of uranium enriched to 60%16. Enriched further, this material would be enough for around ten weapons.

3. No military option to reopen the strait. The IISS concludes that no plausible military option would reopen Hormuz on a lasting basis17. Holding the Iranian coastline would require tens of thousands of troops on the ground, a political cost that Washington rules out. The “Project Freedom” escort operation was suspended in May after protecting only two ships33.

4. Deterrence that no longer deters. Every strike invites retaliation. In mid-August, Tehran proclaimed an “offensive” doctrine, and Mohammad Ghalibaf, the speaker of parliament, has announced an end to proportionate responses. Ali Vaez (Crisis Group) draws out the paradox: the more US economic pressure succeeds, the more it pushes Iran towards force12.

5. Two asymmetric clocks. On the American side, Donald Trump's approval rating has fallen to 32%, a record low (Reuters/Ipsos, 21 September)18. The Senate rejected a resolution limiting the president's war powers by only 50 votes to 49 on 24 September19, and the midterm elections take place on 3 November. On the Iranian side, the regime is betting that it can outlast the Trump presidency, but its economic clock is ticking faster. Mojtaba Khamenei has not appeared in public for more than six months, and power is shifting towards the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC)20.

“Washington is waging a war of logistics; Tehran, a war of sovereignty. Neither ends in the other's defeat.”

03An industrial war of attrition

The decisive question may not be “who is winning?” but “who is exhausted first?” According to CSIS, US stocks of Patriot interceptors fell from around 2,330 before the war to 759–827 in July, and THAAD stocks from 452 to 234–278 (Figure 2)21. These estimates predate the September resumption: on 8 September, a single Iranian salvo of some twenty missiles against a base in Jordan reportedly required between 60 and more than 100 Patriots23.

The asymmetry is, first, economic. A PAC-3 MSE costs about $4m, a THAAD about $15m and a Shahed-136 drone about $50,00022, a ratio of 1 to 80 in the attacker's favour. Second, it is industrial. The United States produces some 600 PAC-3s and 96 THAADs a year, against targets of 2,000 and 40022, and the more than $85bn of framework agreements signed since the summer will not bear fruit until around 2028–203023. The Pentagon puts the cost of the war at $45.1bn as of 18 September, excluding base repairs24.

The opportunity cost is global. The aircraft carrier Lincoln has spent more than 266 days at sea, a record. Its replacement, the George Washington, is based in Japan, which leaves the western Pacific thinly covered25. Moscow is taking advantage. In early September the Financial Times revealed a Russian programme to help develop Iranian supersonic cruise missiles26. The Iranian theatre is becoming a peripheral front of the war in Ukraine: every interceptor fired in the Gulf is one fewer for Kyiv or Taiwan.

04The economic weapon and its limits

The blockade has achieved what sanctions never did. Iranian oil exports fell from about 2 mb/d in March to 740,000 b/d in July, then to 220,000–255,000 b/d in August; 107 million barrels of Iranian crude are stored at sea27. The rial went from 1.35 million to the dollar at the start of 2026 to a record 2.25 million in early September. Annual inflation reached 88.6% in May–June, and the IMF forecasts a 6.1% contraction in GDP in 202628.

On 24 August, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent launched Operation “Economic Outcast”. It comprises some sixty designations, including Chinese nationals, and extends secondary sanctions to five sectors: digital assets, gold, aviation, technology and maritime7. Yet one red line remains: no major Chinese bank is targeted. China buys 80 to 90% of Iran's oil, but Iran accounts for only about 13% of its crude imports29. Beijing can therefore absorb the loss, and Washington is unwilling to sacrifice the Sino-American relationship: Iran was on the agenda of Xi Jinping's state visit from 23 to 25 September30. Finally, the pressure is partly backfiring on its author: the blockade drives up the price of a barrel, and with it the cost of filling up for American voters, six weeks before a national election.

The compliance & sanctions view

What “Economic Outcast” changes for companies

  • The scope is widening. Secondary sanctions risk is no longer confined to energy and banking: gold, crypto-assets, aviation and shipping are now exposed7.
  • The areas requiring vigilance are shifting. Emirati free zones, the Hong Kong and Singapore financial centres, networks of intermediaries (“trustees”) collecting oil revenues outside the banking system.
  • 107 million barrels in floating storage will have to find buyers: ship-to-ship transfers, reflagging and falsified certificates of origin are the typologies to watch.
  • The political limit of “maximum pressure” is well known: as long as the major Chinese banks remain out of scope, the main channel survives.

06Four scenarios to the end of 2026

Central scenario
A frozen, intermittent conflict

This is the prevailing reading among analysts: IRIS speaks of a “frozen conflict”31, Ifri of a “neither peace nor war” situation32, the CFR of a lasting stalemate13. The blockade and the filtering of Hormuz traffic become entrenched; strikes remain occasional.

Indicators: fewer than 20 transits a day through Hormuz, Brent between $90 and $110, sporadic salvoes.

Alternative
An imperfect negotiated pause

A deal that each side can present as a victory: Hormuz reopened in return for the lifting of the blockade, partial unfreezing of assets, return of IAEA inspectors.

Indicators: Qatari and Pakistani shuttle diplomacy, an Iran–Oman text on managing the strait, OFAC licences.

Escalation risk
Regional spillover

A bombing campaign after 3 November, a unilateral Israeli strike on Iranian energy, or a successful Houthi attack on Yanbu.

Indicators: arrival of bombers and aircraft carriers, renewed fire against a carrier, Brent above $120.

Rupture risk
Internal fracturing of the regime

Unlikely as long as the elites remain united. But a significant share of oil revenue funds the IRGC: its loyalty also hinges on pay.

Indicators: the rial beyond 2.5 million to the dollar, fuel shortages, bazaar strikes, defections.
