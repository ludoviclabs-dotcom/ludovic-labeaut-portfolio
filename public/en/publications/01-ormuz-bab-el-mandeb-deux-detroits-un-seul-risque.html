Straits & shipping lanes

Hormuz, Bab el-Mandeb: two straits, one risk

Since February, the crisis can no longer be read strait by strait. The routes that bypass the Gulf have shifted the risk to the Red Sea, where the Houthis now hold Bab el-Mandeb. A map of the chokepoints that matter, of what has given way, and of what markets — and insurers — are already pricing in.

By Ludovic Labeaut9 min readData as of 26 September 2026

Key points

  1. Before the war, around 20 mb/d of oil passed through Hormuz, a fifth of global consumption. From March to May 2026, average flows fell to 2.7 mb/d.
  2. The strait is no longer “open” or “closed”: it has become a filter. Tehran screens ships by flag, issues authorisation codes and levies transit fees.
  3. The bypass routes have not removed the risk but shifted it: Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline ends on the Red Sea, which the Houthis have locked down since seizing Perim Island on 11 September.
  4. The price of risk shows up as much in insurance and freight as in Brent: war-risk premiums up fortyfold, and supertanker charter rates above a million dollars a day.

01Seven chokepoints for one global economy

A strait is not merely a narrow passage: it is a point where geography concentrates flows that nothing can replace in the short term. Seven routes carry the bulk of the world's seaborne oil (figure 1). In the first half of 2025, 20.9 mb/d of crude oil and products passed through Hormuz, around 20% of global consumption1, along with nearly a fifth of the world's traded LNG, mostly Qatari and Emirati2. Malacca, the largest strait by volume (23.2 mb/d), is to a large extent the Asian continuation of that same flow: 84% of the crude leaving Hormuz goes to Asia, including 69% to China, India, Japan and South Korea3.

Physical geography deepens the dependence. At its narrowest point Hormuz is 21 nautical miles wide, but traffic uses two lanes of 2 miles each, separated by a buffer zone1. At Bab el-Mandeb, Perim Island splits the passage into two channels: whoever holds it keeps watch over the strait.

A methodological caveat that specialist readers will spot: the shares vary with the denominator. UNCTAD attributes to Hormuz 34% of seaborne oil exports and 30% of seaborne LNG exports4, whereas the EIA and the IEA put it at around a quarter of seaborne oil trade. Better to name the source than to quote “the” figure.

02Hormuz: from closure to filtering

The war that began on 28 February with US-Israeli strikes (Operation “Epic Fury”) first produced a de facto closure: by 2 March, not a single tanker was showing in the strait's positioning (AIS) data5. On 11 March, the IEA launched the largest release of strategic stocks in its history: 400 million barrels, shared among 32 countries2. From March to May, average flows through Hormuz fell to 2.7 mb/d6.

Yet the defining feature of 2026 is not closure but filtering. From late March, Tehran let through ships selected by flag (China, India, Malaysia, South Korea, Egypt), using authorisation codes issued by the Revolutionary Guards. “Transit fees” of up to $2m per ship, sometimes paid in yuan, have been reported7. In May, Iran institutionalised this control by creating a Persian Gulf Strait Authority8.

This model runs up against the right of transit passage enshrined in the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, a treaty that neither of the two belligerents has ratified, which weakens the legal argument on both sides. Oman, on the strait's southern shore, has notified the UN of its opposition to the tolls8. What followed alternated between truces and relapses:

  • 8 April: ceasefire;
  • 13 April: US blockade of Iranian ports;
  • 17 June: memorandum of understanding guaranteeing 60 days of passage;
  • 6–7 July: strikes resume;
  • 14 July: renewed blockade.

On 28 August, US Central Command (CENTCOM) declared the lanes cleared of mines; Tehran replied that no ship would pass without its authorisation9. On 24 September, Kpler counted only 13 transits over the day10.

The statistical fog. For September, estimates of flows through Hormuz range from 3.7 mb/d (TankerTrackers) to around 10 mb/d (The National), with 6 to 8 mb/d (Bloomberg, from satellite imagery) in between11. The gap owes less to bias in the sources than to method: transponders switched off, ship-to-ship transfers, differing scopes. Any categorical claim about “the” flow through Hormuz should be read with this margin in mind.

03The bypass trap

Gulf producers had a fallback: the pipelines that avoid Hormuz. As early as 28 March, Saudi Arabia raised the capacity of its East-West pipeline, which links its eastern fields to the port of Yanbu on the Red Sea, to 7 mb/d12. The UAE exports around 1.5 mb/d through the ADCOP pipeline to Fujairah, on the Gulf of Oman13.

These routes do not remove the risk; they shift it (figure 2). Fujairah was struck as early as 4 May13. Above all, Saudi crude diverted to Yanbu must then pass through Suez towards Europe or Bab el-Mandeb towards Asia, in other words through the Houthis' area of operations. The escalation followed that path:

  • 20 July: the Houthis declare a naval blockade against Saudi Arabia and strike Saudi tankers in the days that follow14;
  • 10–11 September: a drone attack launched from Iraq forces Riyadh to shut the East-West pipeline, which was then carrying 4 to 5 mb/d15;
  • 11 September: the Houthis seize Perim, then the Hanish and Zuqar islands, and now control the entire Yemeni coastline of the Red Sea16. Traffic through the strait halves within twenty-four hours: 15 transits, against 30 the day before17;
  • 24–25 September: Houthi missiles target Yanbu and Taif; Paris announces the dispatch of troops, radars and defence systems to Yanbu19.

The upshot: Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb no longer form two distinct risks but a single system. Reopening Hormuz would not be enough to normalise flows if the Red Sea remains contested, and vice versa. The figures bear this out. According to Lloyd's List Intelligence, weekly transits through Bab el-Mandeb fell from 319 before 20 July to 202 at the end of August18. Saudi exports reportedly plunged from about 7.5 mb/d at the start of the year to 2.1 mb/d in early September20 (single source, to be cross-checked).

“The bypass routes have not removed the risk: they have shifted it to the Red Sea.”

04Contagion: Suez, Panama, Malacca, the Arctic

Suez, the paradox. Canal revenues rose by 23% in the 2025/26 financial year, to $4.67bn, and by 56.7% year on year in August ($567m)21. They nonetheless remain far below the record $9.4bn of 2022/23: Suez is benefiting from a reshuffling of routes, not from a return to normal.

Panama, the overloaded safety valve. Rainfall 34% below normal from May to August has combined with a surge in US energy exports to Asia. Transits have been capped at 32 a day since 15 September, and a transit slot was auctioned at a record $5.3m on 1st September22. Politically, Panama's Supreme Court annulled CK Hutchison's port concession (Balboa, Cristóbal) at the end of January, and the terminals' management has been handed to Maersk and MSC on an interim basis23 (see note No. 3).

Malacca, calm under watch. The world's busiest strait set a traffic record in 2025, with more than 102,000 transits, and has seen no major incident in 2026. Yet it is where China's vulnerability is concentrated: it is the next flashpoint the region's navies are watching.

The Arctic and the Danish straits. Russia's Northern Sea Route is expected to exceed 40 Mt of cargo in 2026, a first, and the Danish authorities recorded 292 passages through their straits by tankers of Russia's “shadow fleet” in 202524. The US–Denmark–Greenland agreement of 22 September (see note No. 3) puts the GIUK gap, between Greenland, Iceland and the United Kingdom, back at the heart of the Atlantic security architecture.

The markets & insurance view

The price of risk cannot be read in Brent alone

  • Oil. Brent went from about $72 at the end of February to more than $100 by 12 March, then $118 on 31 March; Dated Brent (the physical price) hit a record $144.42 on 7 April25. Having fallen back to around $72 during the early-July truce, it climbed back above $100 on 9 September and closed at $106.6 on the 24th10.
  • Insurance. For a Hormuz transit, the “war risk” premium rose from 0.2–0.25% of the ship's value before the war to 3–10%, according to Marsh (July). For a tanker worth $100m, that amounts to $3m to $10m per transit26. As early as 3 March, the Joint War Committee extended its listed risk areas to Bahrain, Djibouti, Kuwait, Oman and Qatar27.
  • Capital is not the limiting factor. The $40bn US government facility designed to reinsure transits had found no takers by May: shipowners refuse to put their crews at risk28. The constraint is human and military, not financial.
  • Freight. The charter rate for a Gulf–China VLCC (route TD3C) rose from about $50,000/day in mid-2025 to more than $1m/day on 14 and 15 September, a historic first29. At that level, freight weighs more heavily than insurance in the cost of a delivered barrel.
  • Stocks. According to the IEA, global stocks have fallen by 507 million barrels since February30: the buffers of the first half of the year have largely been used up.

For a (re)insurer, the point to watch is accumulation. A single event can now hit hull, cargo, business interruption and trade credit at once, across two straits that have become a single system.

Asia bears the brunt of the physical shock, with very uneven reserves: Japan can hold out for more than 250 days, India for barely 70. Europe, for its part, is exposed less through oil than through gas. The Iranian strike of 18 and 19 March knocked out 17% of Qatar's LNG export capacity for three to five years32, in a market where Doha supplied a significant share of Europe's LNG. The winners are in the Atlantic basin. According to the IEA, US oil exports reached 13.1 mb/d in May6, to the point of saturating Panama.
