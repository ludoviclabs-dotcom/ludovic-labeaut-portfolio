Who owns the blueprints? The reform of US arms procurement

Behind the “affordable” missiles, the real revolution is legal: the Pentagon wants to take back control of its weapons’ interfaces so that its suppliers compete permanently. Open architectures, fixed-price contracts, venture capital: how the reform redistributes the defence industry’s rents, and where it hits its limits.

By Ludovic Labeaut10 min readData as of 26 September 2026

Key points

  1. The heart of the reform is not technological but legal. With open architectures (MOSA, WOSA), the government owns the interfaces and can put every building block of a weapon back out to competition.
  2. The framework shifted decisively in 2025 with the executive order of 9 April, the Hegseth memo of November and the US National Defense Authorization Act (2026 NDAA). Priority now goes to commercial solutions, other transaction agreements (OTAs) and fixed-price contracts.
  3. The new entrants, or “neo-primes”, are capturing the valuations (Anduril at $61bn, Palantir at around $459bn) but still little of the spending: 0.5% of Pentagon contract obligations for the top 100 defence start-ups.
  4. Two risks hang over the reform: replacing one oligopoly with another, and running into the budgetary “valley of death” between prototype and production.

01The problem: “win once, bill forever”

In 2022, a Pentagon report set out the diagnosis: the 51 defence prime contractors of the 1990s have dwindled to just five1. Concentration also extends to critical components. Only two makers of solid rocket motors remain, since the FTC blocked Lockheed’s takeover of Aerojet in 2022 and L3Harris acquired it in 2023 for $4.7bn2.

The mechanism behind the rents fits into a phrase attributed to Will Roper, the US Air Force’s former head of acquisition: “win once, bill forever”3. The prime contractor that wins a programme holds the technical data and the interfaces. Every upgrade, every spare part, every year of sustainment then falls to it without competition.

The F-35 is the textbook case. It was designed in the 1990s under a model that leaves most of the rights with the manufacturer. Its lifecycle cost exceeds $2,000bn. The “Block 4” modernisation is at least five years behind schedule and $6bn over budget, and all 110 aircraft delivered in 2024 arrived late, by 238 days on average4.

02MOSA and WOSA: taking back control of the interfaces

The answer comes with an acronym: MOSA, for Modular Open Systems Approach. Written into law by the 2017 NDAA, then extended in 2021 to all programmes “to the maximum extent practicable”, it requires each system to be broken down into modules linked by published interfaces. The government can obtain the rights to use them5. In January 2019, the three service secretaries described it as an “operational imperative”6. The Macro Insider sums up what is at stake: it is not ownership of the finished product that confers bargaining power, but ownership of the blueprint3.

WOSA (Weapon Open Systems Architecture) applies the same principle to munitions. It is a government-owned standard maintained by the Air Force. The weapon is broken down into modules (guidance, propulsion, warhead, seeker) that exchange standardised messages. The contents of each module remain the property of its manufacturer; only the interface is shared3. On 25 September, Anduril’s Barracuda became the first production munition to be certified to WOSA, across fourteen domains7. The programmes covered in Note No. 7 all mandate this standard: FAMM, MACE and the low-cost interceptor of the Missile Defense Agency (MDA). This is what makes it possible to open a framework agreement to new entrants midway through.

Implementation remains uneven. In January 2025, the GAO, the US counterpart to France’s Cour des comptes, found that 14 of 20 major programmes were applying MOSA, but that none had measured its costs and benefits8. The MV-75 helicopter, successor to the Black Hawk, serves as the showcase: MOSA from the design stage, for a programme worth about $70bn9.

032025–2026: the regulatory tipping point

  • 9 April 2025. Executive Order 14265 sets an order of preference: commercial products, then other transaction agreements (Other Transaction Authority, OTA), then rapid acquisition. It also orders a review of major programmes with overruns of more than 15%10.
  • 7 November 2025. The Hegseth memo “Warfighting Acquisition System” creates portfolio-level acquisition leads (Portfolio Acquisition Executives) and makes “commercial first” and MOSA the default. It explicitly targets the system dominated by prime contractors and “cost-plus” contracts11.
  • 18 December 2025. The 2026 NDAA ($900.6bn) writes these portfolios into law, authorises multi-year procurement of munitions and requires an inventory of technical data12.
  • 7 January and 18 August 2026. An executive order bars underperforming contractors from share buybacks and dividends13. A memo from Deputy Secretary Steve Feinberg then requires transparency on costs and margins14.

Other transaction agreements grew from $1.8bn in 2016 to more than $18bn in 202415. Above all, the government is no longer content merely to buy: it invests. It took a stake of around 15% in MP Materials in July 2025 ($400m of preferred stock, combined with a guaranteed ten-year price floor for rare earths)16. It has also put $1bn into convertible securities in L3Harris Missile Solutions, with a view to an IPO17. These deals will be the subject of the Geoeconomics section.

But valuation is running ahead of volumes. The top 100 defence start-ups received $4.3bn in federal obligations in 2025, or 0.5% of Pentagon obligations22. By way of comparison, Lockheed Martin alone had received $61.4bn in 2023. The incumbent primes, for their part, are adapting without losing their grip. They are forming partnerships (Raytheon with Anduril on rocket motors), investing in start-ups (Lockheed Martin Ventures holds stakes in 14 companies on the list) and posting record order backlogs: $230bn for Lockheed and $289bn for RTX in the second quarter of 202623.

The finance & audit view

What the reform changes in the accounts

  • Two worlds of multiples. Relative to revenue, valuations reach about 56 times for Palantir (expected 2026 revenue) and 28 times for Anduril (2025 revenue), compared with 2.7 times for RTX and 1.5 times for Lockheed (author’s calculations). The market is paying software margins (a 62% adjusted operating margin at Palantir) for businesses that are becoming industrial and capital-intensive.
  • Rents are shifting. By putting each module back out to competition, MOSA attacks the primes’ most profitable segment: the aftermarket and sustainment.
  • Hybrid instruments. Convertible preferred shares with attached warrants (MP Materials, L3Harris Missile Solutions) raise the question of embedded derivatives. The government-guaranteed price floor can be analysed as an implicit put option.
  • Fixed price. The move to fixed prices creates exposure to onerous contracts (IAS 37 provisions, IFRS 15/ASC 606 variable consideration). The precedent of Boeing’s KC-46 tanker, with more than $7bn in losses, is on everyone’s mind.
  • “Firm” backlog or promise? Multi-year framework agreements bind the government only once the funds have been appropriated. The distinction must be made clear in financial reporting.

05The limits of the reform

The valley of death. According to the GAO, only 51% of prototypes funded by the DIU, the Pentagon’s innovation unit, moved into production between 2016 and 202324. The DAWG, successor to Replicator, requested $54.6bn for 2027; congressional committees retained only about $1bn of it (single source). And the continuing resolution passed through 11 December blocks any new programme25.

One oligopoly can replace another. Anduril and Palantir now hold framework agreements worth $10bn to $20bn, and Maven is becoming a programme of record. In the second quarter of 2026, the ten largest funding rounds captured 62% of defence venture capital26. The risk is a shift from dependence on the prime contractor to software dependence.

Inertia. The average time to deliver a new capability exceeds twelve years across the 104 major programmes tracked by the GAO, which are worth $2,400bn27. Declaring an open architecture is easy; imposing it at every renewal is far less so. The right to repair, written into the 2027 NDAA passed by the House, is fiercely contested by industry28.

06And Europe?

The United States supplies 58% of the arms imports of NATO’s European members (2021–2025)29. Every F-35 bought in Europe also imports the closed model described above. The EU is trying to correct this dependence. The SAFE instrument provides €150bn in loans, €16.2bn of which is allocated to France, subject to at least 65% European components. The EDIP programme adds €1.5bn30. But the dynamic is different: in Europe, it is the large groups that back the start-ups (Dassault in Harmattan AI, Saab in Comand AI). In the United States, it is venture capital. In France, only 7.2% of upstream defence studies go to SMEs31. And the European equivalent of MOSA has yet to be written: who will own the interfaces of future collaborative programmes?
