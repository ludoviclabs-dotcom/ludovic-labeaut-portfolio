“Affordable mass”: the Pentagon learns to count again

The war against Iran exposed an untenable equation: $4m interceptors to shoot down drones worth a few tens of thousands of dollars. Washington is answering with a doctrine, “affordable mass”, and with unprecedented multi-year contracts. Here is what it promises, what it really costs and where it hits its limits.

By Ludovic Labeaut10 min readData as of 26 September 2026

Key points

  1. Affordable mass reverses the logic of the past thirty years: fewer “exquisite” munitions, more weapons that are cheaper, produced by the thousand and interchangeable.
  2. It takes concrete form in 2026 through four programmes: FAMM (some 28,000 cruise missiles at $218k over five years), MACE for naval aviation, an interceptor costing under $750k for missile defence, and the LUCAS drone at about $35k.
  3. The sticker price is not the real cost. FAMM was aiming for $100k a unit, MACE for $300k; the actual figures are twice as high. Affordable mass costs roughly a tenth as much as the high end, not a fiftieth.
  4. The real bottleneck is not price but production rate: 42 months to build a PAC-3, two rocket-motor suppliers, a single US producer of ammonium perchlorate.

01Where affordable mass comes from

The idea predates the war. In 2021 the Mitchell Institute argued for a “mix” of guided munitions in which cheap weapons complement high-end ones1. In 2024 the Hudson Institute set out the theory of a “hedge force” made up of low-cost drones2. In between, in August 2023, Deputy Secretary of Defense Kathleen Hicks launched Replicator: “multiple thousands” of “attritable” autonomous systems within 18 to 24 months. By the deadline, hundreds had been delivered, and at the end of 2025 the programme was transferred to a new body, the DAWG3.

The doctrine was then formalised. Pete Hegseth’s memo of 10 July 2025, “Unleashing U.S. Military Drone Dominance”, turned the small drone into an expendable munition4. The Drone Dominance programme (December 2025) plans to buy some 340,000 small drones for $1bn, with a unit cost meant to fall from $5,000 to $2,3005. Finally, the National Defense Strategy of January 2026 placed the industrial base among the Pentagon’s four priorities6.

The war against Iran turned theory into urgency. The reasoning comes down to a cost-exchange ratio: the price of the weapon expended relative to that of the threat destroyed. A PAC-3 MSE at around $4m against a Shahed-136 at $20,000–50,000 is a ratio of at least 80 to 17. As early as 2024, CSIS had warned that this ratio is misleading if it ignores the value of what is being defended: an air base is worth far more than an interceptor8. The fact remains that no stockpile withstands this arithmetic for long.

02Four programmes scaling up

FAMM, the “volume” cruise missile. The US Air Force’s Family of Affordable Mass Missiles seeks not to penetrate defences but to saturate them. It is a subsonic missile with a range of 400 to 800 km, offered in three versions:

  • FAMM-L, launched from a fighter or a bomber;
  • FAMM-P, on pallets dropped from a cargo aircraft;
  • FAMM-BAR, an anti-ship variant with a range of over 1,900 km, still at the request-for-information stage.

On 15 July 2026 the Air Force signed seven-year, fixed-price framework agreements with Anduril (Barracuda-500M), CoAspire (RAACM) and Zone 5 (Rusty Dagger). Minimum orders remain subject to appropriations, and new entrants can still join the programme9. Two of these missiles derive from ERAM, sold to Ukraine in 2025 (3,350 units for $825m)11: the Ukrainian front served as a test bed (see Note No. 9).

MACE, naval aviation’s anti-ship missile. Faced with China’s DF-21D and DF-26 anti-ship ballistic missiles, which force aircraft carriers to stay far from the coast, the US Navy wants a light, fast missile to complement LRASM13. The request for information sought a unit cost of no more than $300,000 and at least 500 units a year. Castelion, with its Blackbeard (over Mach 5), was selected in February 2026 and on 16 September won a $200m production contract for 353 to 444 missiles depending on the source, or roughly $450k to $570k apiece12. A month earlier, the start-up had raised $1bn at a $13bn valuation14.

The Low Cost Interceptor, for missile defence. On 18 August X-Bow Systems announced a $10.98m fixed-price contract from the Missile Defense Agency (MDA), which runs US missile defence. It covers an interceptor with a target cost of under $750,000, against about $4m for a PAC-3 and $12.7m for a THAAD. A flight demonstration is planned for late 202715. What sets X-Bow apart is that it 3D-prints, in-house, the rocket motor in short supply across the entire sector.

Covenant, the “off-the-shelf” missile. Emerging from stealth on 9 September, this start-up founded in 2024 has raised more than $250m (a16z, Founders Fund, Lux…) for a heavy cruise missile, the Anthem, in the Tomahawk class but built largely from civilian components. Its price has not been published. It is aiming for 5,000 units a year in Dallas from the first quarter of 2027 and claims about $150m in orders16.

The same logic is spreading to combat drones. The two models in the CCA programme of uncrewed aircraft that fly alongside fighters, General Atomics’ FQ-42A and Anduril’s FQ-44A, entered production on 17 June 2026, four months ahead of schedule, at an estimated cost of a quarter to a third of an F-35’s17. The LUCAS attack drone, derived from a captured Shahed and priced at around $35,000, was used in combat as early as 28 February18.

03The real price of “low cost”

Affordable mass is subject to the same cost creep as any weapons programme. FAMM aimed for $100,000 a unit in its experimental version; the agreed price is $218,000, and the total budget divided by the number of missiles gives about $450,000 per unit, including integration and support. MACE was supposed to cost $300,000 and actually costs about $500,0001012. These weapons still cost about a tenth as much as a JASSM-ER, but not a fiftieth.

Above all, a cheap shot does not guarantee a cheap effect. CNAS shows this in a study of 18 September: to achieve the same effect, a mixed salvo of 25 cheap missiles and 3 LRASMs costs $14.6m, against $19.2m for a salvo of LRASMs alone. The saving is only a quarter, because far more cheap missiles are needed for the same result19. Other limitations come on top of this:

  • range, against Chinese anti-access bubbles20;
  • the survivability of non-stealthy delivery vehicles;
  • ineffectiveness against hardened targets;
  • reliance on GPS in the face of electronic warfare.

For Stacie Pettyjohn (CNAS), these weapons are complements, not substitutes19.

“Affordable mass does not replace the high end: it buys magazine depth, and therefore time.”

Industry is following, but with its own inertia. On 29 July Lockheed won a $58.62bn multi-year contract for the PAC-3 MSE and is investing $8bn–9bn by 203022; the THAAD contract exceeds $35bn over seven years to quadruple the production rate23. But a PAC-3 takes 42 months to build and a THAAD 53. According to CSIS, it will take a little over three years to get back to pre-war stockpile levels24.

The bottlenecks lie upstream. In 1995 six companies made solid-propellant rocket motors; by 2017 only two remained. It was not until June 2026 that Northrop was qualified as a second source for the PAC-3’s motor25. On 5 August Deputy Secretary of Defense Steve Feinberg gave industry 21 days to submit accelerated production plans for sixteen critical programmes; the responses have not been made public26. Finally, the continuing resolution, which runs until 11 December, bars any new multi-year programme. Without appropriations, framework agreements remain promises27. Venture capital, for its part, is not waiting: Anduril raised $5bn in May at a $61bn valuation28.

The view from Europe & France

Two doctrines of mass

  • An effort changing in scale, but not yet in kind. The updated military programme law, enacted in August, adds €36bn over 2026–2030, including about €8.5bn for munitions29. The FAMM programme alone (≈ $12.6bn over five years) is larger.
  • One Way Effector (MBDA). A remotely operated munition with a range of over 500 km; first batch ordered in January 2026, deliveries in 2027, with a target production rate of up to 1,000 a month30.
  • Chorus (Turgis Gaillard and Renault). An attack drone with a 500 kg payload and a 3,000 km range, at about €100,000–120,000, with capacity of 600 a month at Le Mans. The DGA says it does not want to buy “large quantities” of equipment that quickly becomes obsolete, but rather to guarantee production capacity that can be mobilised31.
  • Harmattan AI. The start-up raised $200m in January (in a round led by Dassault) and delivered 1,000 drones at under €1,000 apiece in less than a year; 5,000 Delco infantry drones have been ordered32.

Two models are emerging. The United States is building up stockpiles, funded by seven-year contracts and venture capital. France is betting on dormant capacity backed by its car industry. It remains to be seen which of the two approaches will better withstand a long war.
