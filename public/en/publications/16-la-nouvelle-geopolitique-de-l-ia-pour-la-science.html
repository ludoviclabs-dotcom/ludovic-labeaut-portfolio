States, Big Tech, universities and industry: the new geopolitics of AI for science

In November 2025, the White House launched the Genesis Mission, a plan to transform US federal research through AI. A few weeks earlier, Brussels had published its strategy for AI in science; London followed with its own. In July 2026, Beijing presented a plan for autonomous laboratories. Behind these strategies lies a shift in power: the most advanced instruments of discovery now belong to a handful of private companies, which set their prices, their conditions of access and sometimes their agenda. This article compares the public strategies and describes how much weight France and Europe can really carry.

By Ludovic Labeaut8 min readState of knowledge as of 8 October 2026

01The question

For decades, the major instruments of science (accelerators, telescopes, supercomputers) were public or international. The most capable AI models are private, mostly American, and subject to Washington’s export controls. How are states taking back control? Are universities becoming customers or partners? And can Europe build a capability of its own without isolating itself?

02The technology, explained

Power in scientific AI rests on a value chain with six links. Each player controls some of them.

03Documented use cases: four public strategies

United States: the Genesis Mission

The executive order of 24 November 2025 tasks the Department of Energy with creating a “closed-loop experimentation platform” integrating supercomputers and federal data, in order to produce scientific foundation models and run robotic laboratories. Priority fields: biotechnology, critical materials, fission and fusion, space, quantum information, semiconductors. Goal: to sharply increase the productivity of federal R&D “within a decade” (White House).

On 18 December 2025, the DOE announced memoranda of understanding with 24 organisations, including Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, NVIDIA, AMD, AWS, xAI, Periodic Labs and Radical AI (DOE). These are statements of intent to collaborate, with no scientific results at this stage. Microsoft then committed $60 million ($40 million in Azure credits, $20 million in services) (Microsoft).

The American model is a public-private partnership in which the state provides data, national laboratories and legitimacy, and companies provide models and cloud. It comes with political control over access: the suspension of Fable 5 and Mythos 5 in June 2026 under export controls (MobiHealthNews) and the restriction of Mythos 5.1 to US organisations (Anthropic) are its concrete expression.

China: municipal and national “AI for Science”

According to the state media outlet ECNS, as reported by Let's Data Science, Beijing published a plan in early July 2026 to become a global hub for AI-assisted research by 2028: autonomous laboratories combining AI, robotics and high-throughput instruments, a general-purpose scientific AI platform, research vouchers. Fields: physics, materials, health, life sciences, quantum, biological breeding. There is a single source and the budgets are not known: the information is unconfirmed in its details. On the model side, Chinese open models (DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, GLM) are among the comparison benchmarks cited by Mistral (Mistral AI): China’s weight comes above all from open weights.

United Kingdom: a mission with numbers attached

The AI for Science strategy of 20 November 2025 devotes up to £137 million, drawn from an AI budget of £2 billion for 2026–2030, to three pillars: data (data from UKRI facilities compliant with the FAIR principles by 2030), compute (access to the AI Research Resource, including Isambard-AI, from 10,000 to 1.4 million GPU hours), skills (at least 1,000 researchers trained). First mission: trial-ready medicines in 100 days by 2030 (techUK).

European Union: RAISE and the gigafactories

The European strategy for AI in science (8 October 2025) rests on RAISE, a “virtual institute” pooling compute, data, talent and funding, and provides €600 million from Horizon Europe for researchers’ access to compute, notably to the gigafactories, as well as a doubling of Horizon Europe’s annual investment in AI to more than €3 billion (European Commission). The RAISE pilot, launched in November 2025, has €33 million (2025 programme) followed by €107 million (2026–2027), with calls on laboratory automation in materials science planned for 2027 (European Commission). The call to select up to seven gigafactories is open until 3 December 2026 (EuroHPC).

European and British public amounts are small compared with the more than $400 billion invested by five large technology companies in 2025 (IEA). The comparison is not direct (infrastructure investment versus research budgets), but it indicates the order of magnitude.

04Big Tech: from supplier to research partner

Companies no longer just sell tools; they organise researchers’ access:

  • OpenAI offers free access to 100,000 researchers until 2027 and claims more than $250 million committed to external research; among the first institutions: the IAS and the ENS (OpenAI).
  • Anthropic is opening 10,000 free or discounted places for academic laboratories and up to $50,000 in credits per project (Anthropic).
  • Google is opening Co-Scientist to researchers via Gemini for Science (Google DeepMind).

These programmes reduce inequalities of access in the short term, but they create habits, dependencies and data flows towards the providers. The Leiden Declaration calls on governments to “regulate the AI industry and invest in public alternatives”, and on industrial collaborations to meet the same standards as academic research (Leiden University).

05Universities and industry

Universities retain the validation function: peer review, replication, training. They are even becoming its bottleneck: mathematicians refuse to validate announcements that come without a paper (Article 5), and arXiv is rationing submissions. On the other hand, they are losing control of the instruments.

On the industry side, strategies diverge: Eli Lilly is building its own AI factory of 1,016 GPUs and opening its models to other biotechs through federated learning (NVIDIA); Isomorphic Labs is multiplying collaborations with large pharmaceutical companies (Isomorphic Labs); start-ups such as Periodic Labs are building private autonomous laboratories (Periodic Labs). Philanthropic organisations (HHMI Janelia, Arc Institute, FutureHouse) occupy an intermediate space, often a more open one.

06What has actually been demonstrated

  • The major powers all adopted an explicit strategy for AI in science between 2025 and 2026 (official documents).
  • Access to the most capable models has become an instrument of US foreign policy (documented fact).
  • Europe has world-class public computing infrastructure (documented fact).

07What remains uncertain

  • The effectiveness of these strategies: none has yet produced a measurable result at national scale.
  • The actual Chinese amounts and how far the Beijing plan has progressed.
  • Europe’s ability to attract frontier models to its gigafactories, or to produce some of its own.
  • The future of international access to Mythos-class models.

09Economic, industrial, public and geopolitical stakes for France

France has rare assets: a player capable of training a large model (Mistral, €3 billion raised in September 2026, according to TechCrunch); Jean Zay and soon Alice Recoque; research organisations (CNRS, CEA, Inserm, Inria) that run programmes such as the PEPR DIADEM on materials (CNRS); health data among the most comprehensive in the world, now hosted by a French provider (Le Monde).

Its weaknesses are well known: modest public budgets, dependence on American chips, fragmented initiatives, and prestigious institutions (the ENS) adopting American tools on an individual basis. Three levers seem within reach:

  1. A collective European negotiation of access to frontier models, with guarantees of continuity.
  2. Targeted investment in high-quality scientific data (including negative results) from major research facilities, which foreign models cannot produce on their own.
  3. Support for specialised open models, where the gap with the giants is small and reproducibility is high.

What you read What it really means
“24 Genesis Mission partners” Memoranda of understanding and intentions, not results

“600 million for scientific AI” Horizon Europe’s contribution to access to compute, notably to the gigafactories
“Free access for researchers” A limited programme, by application or lottery, for a fixed period
“Sovereignty” Can refer to location, applicable law, ownership of the model or ownership of the chips
“Big Tech funds science” Credits and licences, often for its own tools

10Conclusion

The geopolitics of AI for science pits models of organisation against each other more than countries: a public-private partnership under state control in the United States, planning and open models in China, targeted missions in the United Kingdom, pooling and regulation in Europe. No strategy has yet proved itself. For Europe, the risk is not missing out on the race for models, which has already largely been run, but becoming a captive customer of instruments it does not control, while supplying the data and validations that give them their value.
