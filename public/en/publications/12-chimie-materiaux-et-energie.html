Chemistry, materials and energy: predicting, synthesising and validating with autonomous laboratories

In late 2023, two Nature papers made a strong impression: Google DeepMind announced 2.2 million predicted crystals, 380,000 of them stable, and Berkeley’s robotic laboratory A-Lab said it had synthesised 41 new compounds in 17 days. Three years on, this work remains a milestone, but also a textbook case of controversy: solid-state chemists dispute the novelty of many of the materials and the quality of the automated analyses. In the meantime, “autonomous laboratories” have multiplied, funded by start-ups, states and cloud giants. What is a materials prediction worth until the material has been made, characterised and used?

By Ludovic Labeaut9 min readState of knowledge as of 8 October 2026

01The question

In materials science, the path from an idea to a product involves at least four steps: predicting that a compound is stable, synthesising it, verifying that this compound has indeed been obtained, then demonstrating that it has a useful property, at industrial scale. AI has massively accelerated the first step. The question is whether it also accelerates the next three, and at what cost to rigour.

02The technology, explained

The basic tools

Term Definition
DFT (density functional theory) Quantum calculation method that estimates the energy of an arrangement of atoms; costly but standard
Convex hull Boundary of energetically stable compounds: a material “below the hull” should not decompose spontaneously
Graph neural network Model that represents a crystal as a graph of connected atoms, to predict its stability much faster than DFT
Generative model Model that directly proposes new structures with targeted properties
X-ray diffraction (XRD) Technique that identifies the crystal structure of a powder from its diffraction pattern
Rietveld refinement Method of fitting the XRD pattern to determine the exact composition of a sample
Autonomous laboratory (self-driving lab) Laboratory in which software chooses the next experiment based on previous results and robots carry it out

The discovery loop

Periodic Labs, a US start-up, neatly sums up the loop it is aiming for: formulating a target from stability and property predictions, predicting how to synthesise it, then determining what has actually been made and whether it has the desired properties (Periodic Labs). The third step, characterisation, is the most neglected in announcements and the most criticised by specialists.

03Documented use cases

GNoME: prediction at scale

According to Google DeepMind (29 November 2023), GNoME, a graph neural network trained on Materials Project data, proposed 2.2 million new crystals, 380,000 of which lie on the final convex hull, after DFT verification. The company states that 736 of these structures have been made experimentally by outside teams. Level: P for the prediction method (Nature), BI for the interpretation (“800 years of knowledge”).

Criticism: Anthony Cheetham and Ram Seshadri (UC Santa Barbara) examined a random sample of these structures in Chemistry of Materials (2024) and cast doubt on the novelty and usefulness of many entries (404 Media). The debate is less about the calculations than about what should be called a “new material”.

A-Lab: robotic synthesis and the challenge to it

The Nature paper (November 2023) reports that the A-Lab at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory obtained 41 of 58 targets in 17 days and 355 experiments. Details often omitted: 35 of the 41 successes used literature-inspired recipes; only 37% of the 355 recipes produced their target; slow kinetics explains 11 of the 17 failures, and the algorithm could neither regrind nor heat for longer.

In March 2024, Josh Leeman, Leslie Schoop (Princeton), Robert Palgrave (University College London) and their colleagues published a reanalysis in PRX Energy. According to Chemistry World, they fault the system for ignoring compositional disorder: in Palgrave’s words, “two-thirds of their predictions were simply ordered versions of compounds already known to be disordered”. They also criticise the automated Rietveld refinement. Palgrave’s conclusion: “it is likely that they made no discoveries”. Gerbrand Ceder, who led the project, responded by publishing additional data that he considers confirm the syntheses. Susan Latturner (Florida State University) believes that “a competent human” is still needed to determine what has actually been made.

This debate does not discredit the automation of synthesis, which works. It shows that the interpretation step, entrusted to AI, was the weak link.

MatterGen: generating rather than filtering

Microsoft Research published MatterGen in Nature in January 2025 (doi 10.1038/s41586-025-08628-5): a generative model that proposes inorganic structures according to targeted properties (composition, symmetry, mechanical or magnetic properties). The paper includes a proof-of-concept experimental synthesis; this is a single example, not a statistical validation.

The autonomous laboratories of 2026

Actor What is announced Level of evidence Source

Anthropic (Model Hardware Standard) Connection standard between models and instruments; at Carnegie Mellon, drivers and orchestration written in about 8 hours, six deliberately induced hazardous situations blocked before any movement, experiments about three times faster BI, proofs of concept not evaluated Anthropic, 27 August 2026
Mistral AI Demonstration of a complete Hartree–Fock simulation generated by Large 4 BI (demonstration) Mistral AI

An instructive example of Anthropic’s standard: at Carnegie Mellon, the agent rejected a saturated measurement on its own initiative (R² coefficient below 0.9), reran the experiment at half the concentration and accepted a fit with R² above 0.98 (Anthropic). This is exactly the quality-control task that the A-Lab lacked in 2023. But it is a demonstration chosen by the company.

Nature’s feature “Inside the self-driving lab revolution” (April 2026) takes stock of this movement and its implications for careers (Nature/PubMed).

Energy

Energy applications are the main argument for funding: batteries, ionic conductors, catalysts, materials for fusion. GNoME claims 528 potential lithium-ion conductors (Google DeepMind); Microsoft says it cuts the analysis of data on energy-storage materials “from years to weeks” (Microsoft). These figures are predictions or claims: none of the sources consulted documents an AI-discovered material deployed industrially in a commercial battery (information not confirmed).

05What remains uncertain

  • Novelty: is a substituted or doped compound a “new material”? The community has no shared convention.
  • Synthesisability: stable according to DFT does not mean manufacturable under realistic conditions.
  • Negative data: synthesis failures are rarely published, which biases model training. Autonomous laboratories produce these failures systematically; it remains to be seen whether they will be shared or kept as a competitive advantage.
  • Reproducibility: private systems (Periodic Labs, Microsoft Discovery) publish neither their data nor complete protocols.
  • Scale-up: between a few grams and industrial production, the obstacles are mainly ones of process, not of prediction.

06Operational architecture: an autonomous materials laboratory

07Economic, industrial, public and geopolitical stakes

The sums remain modest compared with those for language models, but the logic is strategic: magnets without rare earths, batteries without critical metals, catalysts for hydrogen. In the United States, Periodic Labs and Radical AI are among the 24 organisations that have signed agreements with the Department of Energy for the Genesis Mission (DOE). In the United Kingdom, the AI for Science strategy provides for a call to develop autonomous laboratory platforms and cites Liverpool’s Materials Innovation Factory (techUK). In China, the Beijing municipal government published a plan in July 2026 aiming for autonomous laboratories combining AI, robotics and high-throughput instruments by 2028; single-source information (state media outlet ECNS), details not confirmed (Let's Data Science).

In France, the main vehicle is the exploratory PEPR DIADEM, co-led by the CNRS and the CEA, with funding of around €85 million over eight years since 2022, which structures four platforms for synthesis, high-throughput characterisation, simulation and AI-based data exploitation (CNRS). It draws on the large-scale facilities SOLEIL and ESRF. The CNRS has set the goal of cutting the identification cycle for a material from 20 years to 4 to 10 years (CNRS, list of PEPRs): this is a goal, not a result. We found no public source documenting a closed-loop autonomous laboratory on the scale of A-Lab in France (information not confirmed).

Two European assets deserve emphasis: the density of large-scale facilities (synchrotrons, neutron sources), which provide high-quality characterisation data, and a solid-state chemistry culture that has produced precisely the most robust criticisms. The main risk is that the data from these public infrastructures will mainly serve to train foreign private models.

A final issue is security: chemistry shares a dual-use risk with biology (toxic molecules, explosives). Anthropic’s standard incorporates hardware interlocks, which is a step in the right direction, but no public standard yet governs the connection of AI agents to laboratory instruments.

Box

What not to confuse

What you read What it really means
“380,000 new materials” 380,000 structures calculated to be stable; most of them not made
“Synthesised by a robot” A product has been obtained; it remains to be proven that it is the target compound
“New compound” Sometimes an ordered or substituted variant of a known compound
“Autonomous laboratory” Autonomous over one specific sequence of steps; human oversight still needed for interpretation
“Battery material” Predicted candidate; no documented industrial deployment
“Trillion-parameter model for XRD” Company announcement with no published evaluation

08Conclusion

AI has solved the problem of producing candidates in abundance and is starting to automate synthesis. The bottleneck has shifted to characterisation and interpretation: knowing what has really been made. This is where the A-Lab controversy is valuable, and this is where human expertise, large public facilities and the sharing of negative data remain decisive. For Europe, the comparative advantage lies less in the model than in the instrument and high-quality data.
