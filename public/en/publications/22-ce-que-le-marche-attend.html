What “the Market Expects” Means: A Date, an Instrument, Assumptions

On 14 October 2026, the press cited, based on the Chicago exchange's (CME) FedWatch tool, a 41.5% probability that the Federal Reserve (Fed) would raise its rates on 28 October. On the morning of the 28th, the same tool, cited by another source, showed only 7.2%. The Fed held its rates, with two votes for a hike, and a December hike was still priced at about 83% after the press conference. So what did “the market” expect? The phrase means nothing without a date, an instrument and assumptions. This article shows how a price becomes a probability, what the calculation assumes, why European and Japanese figures vary by source, and what separates a price from a survey or a projection, for rates as for inflation.

By Ludovic Labeaut10 min readState of knowledge as of 31 October 2026

01A hike probability that fell from 41.5% to 7.2% in two weeks

The Fed's Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) held its target range at 3.75–4.00% on 28 October, by 10 votes to 2: Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack wanted a 25 basis point (bp) hike, or 0.25 percentage point. Article 1 looks back at the decision. The table brings together the probabilities published by the press, all attributed to CME FedWatch, a tool based on futures contracts on the federal funds rate.

Date cited Meeting in question Probability of a hike Source
8 Oct. 28 Oct. 19% Reuters, via Investing.com
14 Oct. 28 Oct. 41.5% Yahoo Finance
28 Oct., morning 28 Oct. 7.2% Schwab
28 Oct., after the press conference December About 83% Schwab

Over three weeks, an October hike was thus priced at 19%, then at 41.5% on the day the September price index was published, then at 7.2%. These figures do not contradict each other: they do not date from the same day, and an expectation without a date cannot be compared with anything. All the market probabilities cited here are dated press quotations, based on their producers' tools (CME FedWatch, Bloomberg): none has been recalculated from prices, for lack of free access to time-stamped quotes.

The figure comes from a short calculation. Under the CME rulebook, the 30-day futures contract settles at 100 minus the average, over the contract month, of the effective federal funds rate (EFFR), published daily by the New York Fed. Take a fictitious example: the EFFR stands at 3.88%, its actual value on 5 October, and the November 2026 contract trades at 95.98. The implied November rate is 100 − 95.98 = 4.02%. If a hike lifts the EFFR by exactly 25 bp, the gap of 0.14 percentage point represents 0.14 / 0.25 = 0.56 of a hike: a probability of 56%.

The choice of contract matters. The 28 October decision takes effect on the 29th: the October contract reflects it on only 3 days out of 31, and a 1 bp error in its price is multiplied more than tenfold in the probability. November has no meeting, the next one being on 8–9 December: its implied rate directly gives the rate expected after the decision. FedWatch follows this logic and extends it to several meetings through a scenario tree, according to its methodology, without removing any premium.

02The calculation rests on five assumptions, and a 5 bp premium changes the verdict

This calculation is exact only under five assumptions. The first is risk neutrality: the price would be the expected value of the rate, with no premium. The second limits the outcomes to two, status quo or +25 bp; if the market also contemplates +50 bp or a cut, the same average corresponds to several distributions. The third assumes a stable spread between the EFFR and the target range: the EFFR sits near its midpoint (3.88% on 5 October as on 30 October), but a 2 bp drift skews the probability by 8 points. The last two rule out any decision between meetings and require an exact calendar, month-ends and public holidays included.

The first is the most fragile. Piazzesi and Swanson (2008) show that federal funds futures contain a time-varying risk premium, larger at longer horizons. Suppose, in the fictitious example, a premium of 5 bp: the expected rate becomes 4.02 − 0.05 = 3.97%, and the probability (3.97 − 3.88) / 0.25 = 0.36. A 5 bp premium takes the probability from 56% to 36%, from a “likely” hike to an “unlikely” one. Yet the October figures are quoted to a tenth of a point: that precision belongs to the calculation, not to the measurement.

In the euro area and Japan, figures that depend on the source

For the European Central Bank (ECB) and the Bank of Japan (BoJ), the reasoning relies on overnight indexed swaps (OIS), which exchange a fixed rate for a compounded overnight rate, €STR in the euro area and TONA in Japan, for want of an equivalent liquid futures contract. Above all, the ECB has no public tool equivalent to FedWatch.

On 5 October, FXStreet wrote that money market rates were pricing in 0.28 of a hike for 29 October and 0.89 by 17 December, without naming the data provider; 0.28 of a hike becomes “28%” only if one assumes just two outcomes. For the BoJ, the press cited about a 12% chance of a hike on 30 October based on OIS (Tradingpedia, 7 October, Bloomberg data), and 26% the day before based on futures contracts (FXStreet): two instruments, two days, a twofold gap.

03Projections, surveys and prices do not answer the same question

The word “expectation” covers three objects: policymakers' projections, drawn up under their own assumptions; surveys, which collect a stated central scenario, with no risk premium; and prices, which blend expectation and premium.

The Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) of 16 September 2026 brings together the views of 18 FOMC participants. On its dot plot, 16 put the end-2026 rate at 4.125% or higher (midpoint of the range), including four at 4.375%. The median rises from 3.8% in June to 4.1%, i.e. one more hike by December. But each dot is conditional on the policy that participant judges appropriate, and not all 18 vote: the median is neither a decision nor a forecast of the Committee. The minutes published on 7 October say that “most participants” judged a further hike likely to be appropriate by the end of the year; these quantifiers (“most”, “several”, “a few”) follow a customary order, with no published numerical definition. After the October hold, only the 8–9 December meeting can still deliver the median path.

The ECB's projections are staff projections, not endorsed by the Governing Council, and rest on interest rate assumptions derived from markets, set as of 19 August 2026 for the September projections: they do not say what the ECB will do, they incorporate what the market expected on that date. The BoJ's Outlook report gives the median of Policy Board members' forecasts: the 30 October report raises its forecast for inflation excluding fresh food in fiscal 2027 to 2.6% (2.4% in July).

The New York Fed's survey of market participants for July 2026 (63 responses out of 65) projected no hike until April 2027: the 16 September hike therefore came as a surprise. In the euro area, a Reuters poll cited by Admiral Markets on 28 October found 64 of 73 economists expecting a hike in December. For the Fed, the table sets the three objects against one another on the same question: a further hike by the end of 2026.

Object Answer Date Nature
Participants' projections (SEP) Median at 4.1%: one more hike 16 Sept. Conditional individual views
Survey of market participants (New York Fed) Range midpoint at 3.63%: no hike July Stated central scenario
Futures contracts (CME FedWatch, cited by Schwab) December hike priced at about 83% 28 Oct. Price with no premium removed

These figures do not contradict each other: they have neither the same author, nor the same date, nor the same nature. Setting the dot plot median against a price means comparing conditional views with an expected value plus a premium.

Inflation: market prices and households do not see the same thing

A breakeven inflation rate is the gap between the yield on a conventional government bond and that on an inflation-indexed bond of the same maturity, TIPS in the United States. The “5-year, 5-year forward” measure, derived from the 5- and 10-year breakevens, isolates the distant horizon, the one that matters for anchoring. The table contrasts these prices with household surveys.

5-year breakeven Market price 2.37% 6 Oct.
10-year breakeven Market price 2.36% 7 Oct.

Households, New York Fed, 1 / 3 / 5 years Survey 3.6 / 3.2 / 3.0% August, published 8 Sept.
Households, Michigan, 1 year / long term Survey 4.6 / 3.4% September, final

Even at a comparable horizon, the gap remains clear: 3.0% at five years for the New York Fed's households, 2.37% for the breakeven. The simplest reading, markets that judge distant inflation to be anchored and households that react to energy prices, calls for two caveats. Besides expected inflation, a breakeven contains an inflation risk premium, minus a liquidity premium on indexed securities, which is significant in the early years of TIPS and in times of crisis (D'Amico, Kim and Wei, 2018): a falling breakeven may reflect a flight to liquidity. On the household side, survey expectations depart from rational expectations (Coibion, Gorodnichenko and Kamdar, 2018): households extrapolate from the prices they see, food and fuel, and their answers are widely dispersed.

Precedent: May–June 2013, when words drove rates up

At the start of the taper tantrum, the Fed was buying $45 billion of Treasury securities and $40 billion of mortgage-backed securities each month (amounts inferred from the FOMC statement of 18 December 2013). On 22 May 2013, the prepared text of Ben Bernanke's testimony before Congress's Joint Economic Committee said only that the Committee was “prepared to increase or reduce the pace of its asset purchases”. The sentence that became famous, about a possible reduction within the next few meetings, came from the question-and-answer session and is known only through the press (Reuters, 23 May 2013), not from an official transcript. On 19 June, at his press conference, he judged it “appropriate to moderate the monthly pace of purchases later this year”.

The 10-year Treasury yield rose 132 bp between 1 May and 5 September (US Treasury daily closes), in two stages aligned with these remarks. On 18 September, the absence of a reduction, a surprise in the opposite direction, pushed it down 17 bp in a single session. The reduction was not decided until 18 December: $40 billion and $35 billion a month, respectively, from January 2014.

Expectations therefore moved months before the decision, in response to words. Closing-level changes do, however, mix in other news of the day, and the split between expected short-term rates and the term premium is a matter for article 6.

04What remains uncertain

  • The size of the premium. Piazzesi and Swanson establish that it varies, not that a simple adjustment is enough: we do not know whether 7.2% underestimated or overestimated the probability investors had in mind. A survey of market participants on the same date would be needed; the September results of the New York Fed's survey had not been published as of 8 October.
  • The gap between prices and the dot plot. If the December pricing and the SEP median diverge, one reading will see a market that doubts the Fed, another two different objects; the distribution of the dots helps decide between them.
  • The meaning of inflation expectations. A rising breakeven may signal de-anchoring or shifting premiums; households' 4.6% at one year, second-round effects or the extrapolation of visible prices. A model that separates out the premiums, and the trend in surveys rather than a single data point, help settle the question.

05What to watch

  • The minutes of the 27–28 October FOMC meeting, published about three weeks after the decision: their quantifiers will show whether the two dissenters were isolated.
  • The Fed's 8–9 December meeting and its SEP: the decision will test the roughly 83% pricing cited on 28 October, and the dot plot will show whether the end-2026 median stays at 4.1%.
  • The ECB's 16–17 December meeting and the Eurosystem staff projections: it will show whether the 64 of 73 economists in the Reuters poll were right.
  • The BoJ's 17–18 December meeting: it will show whether the “slightly better than even” chances cited on 28 October (Reuters, via Admiral Markets) and Hajime Takata's dissent in favour of 1.5% foreshadowed a hike.
  • The New York Fed surveys: the survey of market participants, conducted before each FOMC meeting and published with a lag, will make it possible to compare a stated answer and a price for the same meeting; the monthly consumer survey will show whether one-year expectations are receding.
