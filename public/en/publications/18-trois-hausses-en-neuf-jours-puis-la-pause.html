Three Hikes in Nine Days, Then a Pause: One Move, Three Diagnoses

On 10 September 2026, the ECB raised its rates by 25 basis points (bp), or 0.25 percentage point. The Fed followed on 16 September, the Bank of Japan (BoJ) on the 18th. From 28 to 30 October, all three held their rates. From a distance, the sequence looks coordinated: joint tightening in the face of an energy shock, then a joint pause. The official documents tell a different story. Votes, choice of words and projections reveal three distinct diagnoses and three ways of talking about what comes next. This article goes back over both sequences, text by text: what each document says, what it does not say, and why the vote count says more than the statement about the December decisions.

By Ludovic Labeaut11 min readState of knowledge as of 31 October 2026

01September: one move, three ways of talking about what comes next

For the Fed and the ECB, these hikes break with the cuts of 2024–2025: the Fed had started cutting on 18 September 2024, the ECB on 6 June 2024. The BoJ, for its part, is pursuing a rise begun on 19 March 2024, when it exited its negative rate (official statements).

Based on the statements of the ECB, the Fed and the BoJ, the table summarises the three decisions and their forward guidance, that is, what each bank says about its future decisions.

The move is identical, the stance is not: the ECB refuses any commitment, the Fed reveals a majority for going further, the BoJ announces that it will continue.

The Fed: the hike could be read in the July vote

On 29 July 2026, the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), which sets the Fed's rates, held its target range at 3.50–3.75% by 9 votes to 3 (statement of 29 July). The presidents of the Cleveland, Minneapolis and Dallas Feds (Hammack, Kashkari, Logan) already wanted 25 bp more. On 16 September, the hike passed unanimously, justified by a “timelier” return of inflation to 2%. The July vote did not cause this decision, since both were responding to the same data; it foreshadowed it.

The Summary of Economic Projections (SEP) published the same day says more than the statement. Of 18 participants, four expect two further hikes by December, twelve just one, two none; the median rate for end-2026 rises from 3.8% in June to 4.1% (SEP of 16 September). This dot plot aggregates individual views, each based on the policy its author judges appropriate: it is neither a Committee forecast nor a commitment.

The minutes, an anonymised summary of the discussion published three weeks after the meeting, confirmed on 7 October that “most participants” judged a further hike likely to be appropriate by the end of the year, depending on the data (minutes of 15–16 September).

The ECB: acting without committing

The deposit facility rate, the rate paid to banks on their deposits at the central bank, rose to 2.50% from 16 September, after a first hike in June and a hold in July. Christine Lagarde described the decision as “unanimous”; since the Governing Council's votes are not published, the word is hers. The text repeats the “data-dependent and meeting-by-meeting” approach. At the press conference, the President said she did not yet see second-round effects, that is, the shock spreading to wages and other prices (statement and Q&A of 10 September).

The staff projections published the same day shed light on this choice. Headline inflation would fall from 3.0% in 2026 to 2.1% in 2028, but core inflation, which excludes the most volatile components, would rise from 2.5% in 2026 to 2.6% in 2027 (September projections, assumptions cut off on 19 August). These are staff projections, which the Governing Council does not endorse.

The BoJ: a reasoned dissent, a conditional promise

On 18 September, the Policy Board, the BoJ's decision-making body, raised its policy rate, the uncollateralised overnight call rate, to “around 1.25 percent” by 7 votes to 2. The dissenters were named and gave their reasons: Asada Toichiro judged that inflation excluding fresh food, at below 2%, did not prove the economy's strength; Sato Ayano saw no clear acceleration. The statement considers that underlying inflation “has been approaching 2 percent” and warns that the Bank “will continue to raise the policy interest rate” if its outlook is realised. This is the only explicit guidance from the three institutions.

The Summary of Opinions of 1 October, an unattributed summary of members' remarks, shows a divided Policy Board (Summary of Opinions). Some members judge the target already reached in trend terms, others do not; some want to move “relatively early” towards the intended level, others reject any haste. Without attribution, the weight of each camp remains unknown.

02October: three pauses, with voices for going higher still

Between the two sequences, the September statistics show inflation driven by energy in the United States and the euro area. In the United States, the consumer price index (CPI) rose 3.7% year on year, its energy component 20.4% (BLS, 14 October). The personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, the one the Fed targets, reached 3.6%; published on 29 October, it arrived the day after the Fed's decision (BEA, 29 October). In the euro area, the harmonised index (HICP) rose 3.8%, energy 18.9%, the index excluding energy 2.3% (Eurostat, 16 October, final figure).

Japan is the exception: there, the headline index (+2.1%) remains below the index excluding fresh food and energy (+2.4%) (Statistics Bureau, 23 October). The Japanese question is less about energy than about underlying inflation. These figures are observations, subject to revision; article 2 of this series compares these indices.

Three holds, then, but not three identical signals.

At the Fed, Beth Hammack, a dissenter in July, again called for a hike, joined by Lisa Cook; Kashkari and Logan were not among the dissenters. Of the two changes to the text, the first describes a stronger economy, the second justifies waiting. No projections are published before December.

Expectations had swung widely. According to the press, citing the CME FedWatch tool, which derives probabilities from futures contract prices, a hike was priced at 41.5% on 14 October, after the CPI (Yahoo Finance), then at 7.2% on the morning of the 28th (Schwab). After the press conference of Kevin Warsh, Fed Chair since 22 May 2026, a December hike was still priced at about 83%, according to the same source. These figures have not been recalculated; article 5 of this series explains what they assume.

At the ECB, the energy-related balance of risks sums up the tension inherent in a supply shock, which pushes prices up and slows activity at the same time. The debate on a hike, acknowledged by the President, ended in a “unanimous” decision, according to her. Before the meeting, 64 of the 73 economists in a Reuters poll expected a hike in December (cited by Admiral Markets, 28 October): a survey cited second-hand, not a market price. On 29 October, the day of the decision, the ECB announced Christine Lagarde's departure on 7 February 2027, without giving a reason, although her term ran until 31 October 2027 (press release of 29 October). Article 3 deals with the succession.

At the BoJ, the dissent switched sides. In September, two members found the hike premature; on 30 October, Hajime Takata wanted to go to 1.5%, judging that underlying inflation had reached the target. The Outlook report, the median of Policy Board members' forecasts, raised expected inflation excluding fresh food for fiscal year 2027 from 2.4% (July) to 2.6%. It judges underlying inflation broadly consistent with 2%, with risks skewed to the upside (October Outlook). The BoJ also decided, unanimously, to increase its sales of ETF units, bought during its years of easing, from about 330 bn to 860 bn yen a year from January 2027.

03What the comparison teaches: the vote, the silence and the status of projections

Set side by side, these documents bring out three findings.

The vote says more than the text. At the Fed, the July dissents foreshadowed the hike that the statement left unsaid; in October, two votes for a hike make a hold something other than the status quo. At the BoJ, the direction of dissent reversed in six weeks. A dissent makes a hike more likely, without guaranteeing it. The ECB does not publish its votes: its account, about four weeks after each meeting, gives the range of positions without names or a count.

The absence of guidance is a message. The ECB repeats “meeting-by-meeting”; one possible reading is that it is keeping its freedom in the face of a shock of unknown duration. The Fed gives no direction in its statements and in effect refers readers to the SEP and the minutes. The BoJ promises to continue, conditionally. These are three degrees of commitment, from explicit refusal to conditional promise.

The projections cannot be compared like for like. The figures look alike, the objects do not, as this table drawn from the documents already cited shows.

Projection Author What it is not Latest figure cited
SEP (Fed) 18 participants, each according to the policy they judge appropriate Neither a Committee forecast nor a commitment Median rate end-2026: 4.1% (16 Sept.)
Projections (ECB) ECB or Eurosystem staff, with market-derived rates The Governing Council's view HICP 2026: 3.0% (10 Sept.)
Outlook (BoJ) Policy Board members, as medians The full dispersion of views Fiscal 2027: 2.6% (30 Oct.)

A median of individual views, a staff exercise and a median of voting members do not have the same status; the indices also differ, and the BoJ reasons in fiscal years. None of these projections is a decision.

The resulting method comes down to two steps: date the expectation before the meeting and specify its nature (market price, survey or projection); then separate the fact, the interpretation attributed to its author and the dated market reaction. Without a date, “the market expected” means nothing.

Precedent: 2022, acting early against a supply shock?

In 2022, the Fed and the ECB began raising their rates three months before inflation peaked. The Fed raised its target range to 0.25–0.50% on 16 March 2022 (FOMC statement); the CPI peaked in June at 9.1%, its largest twelve-month increase since November 1981 (BLS, 13 July 2022). The ECB raised rates by 50 bp on 21 July 2022; the HICP peaked at 10.6% in October (Eurostat, 17 November 2022). There followed four 75 bp hikes at the Fed in 2022 and 450 bp of hikes at the ECB through September 2023 (ECB Annual Report 2025). Having stayed at −0.1% until March 2024, the BoJ drops out of the comparison.

The question of whether the reaction came too late remains open: the competing readings of this inflation (demand, supply, energy) do not settle it.

In September 2026, the Fed and the ECB raised their rates with inflation already above target. Seeing this as a lesson from 2022, reacting early to a supply shock to forestall second-round effects, is a hypothesis. On 10 September, Christine Lagarde said she did not yet see these effects: the hike preceded them, which is consistent with the hypothesis without proving it. Minutes, accounts and speeches citing 2021–2022 will make it possible to test it. The strategies revised in 2025 (ECB on 30 June, Fed on 22 August) lend themselves to two readings: a return to a more symmetric reaction after 2021–2023, or mere adjustments of vocabulary (article 3).

04What remains uncertain

  • Pause or end of cycle. The dissents, the median of the September SEP and the BoJ's raised forecast point towards further hikes; the Fed's new sentence and the ECB's refusal to set a path leave open a longer wait. None of these signals is a commitment.
  • The nature of the shock. For the ECB, it remains to be seen whether energy will spread to other prices; for the BoJ, whether underlying inflation has reached 2%, which Takata argues and the September dissenters disputed.
  • The sources' blind spots. The ECB's votes remain unknown, and its September account, published on 8 October, is not used here, nor are the Fed's press conferences. The expectations for December cited here all come from the press.

05What to watch

  • Fed, 8–9 December, with a new SEP. A hike would deliver the September median (4.125%, rounded to 4.1%, i.e. 25 bp above the current midpoint of the range, 3.875%); the vote will show whether the dissents spread.
  • ECB, 16–17 December, with the Eurosystem staff projections. They will show whether projected core inflation for 2027 (2.6% in September) is revised, and the decision whether the October debate turns into a hike.
  • BoJ, 17–18 December. The vote will show whether Takata's position gains support, the statement whether the conditional promise holds. No Outlook in December: the next one comes out in January.
  • The October records: Fed minutes about three weeks after the meeting, the BoJ's Summary of Opinions one to two weeks after, the ECB's account about four weeks after. They will give the range of views and make it possible to test the hypothesis of a lesson drawn from 2022.
  • The appointment of Christine Lagarde's successor by the European Council (Article 283 TFEU), before her departure on 7 February 2027.
