Sanctions on Iran: dollar power put to the test by China

Washington wants to cut off the resources that allow Tehran to finance its power. But this strategy runs into a limit: reaching Iran’s main trading partner would force the United States to risk a far broader economic confrontation with China.

By Ludovic Labeaut4 min readData as of 26 September 2026

On 24 August 2026, Scott Bessent dressed a financial campaign in the language of an amphibious landing. With his “Economic D-Day”, the Treasury Secretary launched Operation Economic Outcast. The stated objective is to isolate the Iranian regime and dry up the resources that sustain it, notably those of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC). Economic pressure is meant to accompany military action and weigh on Tehran’s choices. This is a political ambition; announcing it does not prove that it will be achieved. [1]

The package extends an arsenal that already existed. The Treasury announced nearly sixty designations of individuals, entities and vessels, as well as an extension of sanctions risk to five Iranian sectors: digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping. Behind this diversity lies a single logic: preventing oil revenue from turning into available financing. [1]

01Payment, the second frontier of trade

An international sale does not end when a ship delivers its cargo. Payment still has to be made, and the funds converted and made usable. Washington seeks to intervene all along this chain.

So-called primary sanctions govern persons and transactions subject to US jurisdiction. Secondary sanctions extend the pressure to certain foreign actors: a bank may face restrictions on its correspondent accounts in the United States if it knowingly facilitates certain specified significant transactions. These accounts give access to another bank’s services, notably for international settlements. The lever, then, is to make a relationship with Iran too costly relative to the business the intermediary wants to protect. [2]

This pressure is taking concrete form. On 14 September OFAC, the US agency responsible for administering economic sanctions, added an Iran-related designation against Russia’s VTB Bank, already sanctioned on other grounds. The Treasury accuses it in particular of having developed relationships with sanctioned Iranian banks. Two days later FinCEN convened international financial institutions to share information on Iranian financing networks. The campaign thus mobilises compliance departments as much as diplomacy. [2][3]

02China, an indispensable partner and a costly target

Beijing’s importance is long-standing. According to the US Energy Information Administration, China took nearly 90% of Iran’s crude and condensate exports in 2023, based on tanker tracking. This historical benchmark does not measure flows in September 2026, but it shows how concentrated Iran’s outlets have become. [4]

Yet it would be inaccurate to say that Washington spares all Chinese actors. The 24 August salvo also targets intermediaries based in China and Hong Kong. What Washington holds back from is a far broader confrontation, notably with the major Chinese banks. MacroInsider highlights this discrepancy in its analysis of 26 August. On 21 September Reuters was still observing that the general threats against Iran’s partners did not appear to have been applied to China in full. [1][5][6]

This caution reflects the potential cost of escalation. Hitting a major Chinese bank could disrupt payments unrelated to Iran. Beijing also has industrial means of retaliation, notably over rare earths. Finally, Washington is seeking to preserve trade negotiations with China. Our reading, therefore, is one of selective pressure: making assistance to Tehran harder without triggering a rupture whose consequences the United States itself would bear. [5][6]

Dialogue does not erase disagreement. On 26 September Reuters reported that at the UN, Chinese Vice-President Han Zheng defended respect for the sovereignty of the Gulf states, following the Trump–Xi summit. The confrontation over Iran thus continues to coexist with exchanges at the highest level. [7]

03Choking off revenue does not guarantee a concession

Success should be measured by the resources actually made unavailable and the decisions obtained from Tehran, rather than by the number of sanctions alone. A transaction can disappear, become more expensive or move to another intermediary: these outcomes do not carry the same weight.

The cost can also reach civilian activity. Humanitarian exemptions exist, subject to conditions, notably for food and medicine; their legal existence does not guarantee that a bank will accept every transaction. [8] For a French company, the issue is therefore also a practical one: a commercial contract depends on the availability of its payment, transport and insurance channels.

The “Economic D-Day” thus reveals a central tension in geoeconomics. Financial integration gives Washington a powerful means of pressure. That same integration raises the price of a confrontation with an actor the size of China.
