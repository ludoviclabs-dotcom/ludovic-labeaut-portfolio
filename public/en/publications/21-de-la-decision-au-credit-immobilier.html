From the Decision to the Mortgage: What a 25 Basis Point Hike Really Changes

Between 10 and 18 September 2026, the ECB, the Fed and the Bank of Japan (BoJ) raised their policy rates by 25 basis points (bp), or 0.25 percentage point, before holding them at the end of October. Money market rates followed within days; household credit runs on a different clock: in the week of 1 October, a 30-year fixed-rate US mortgage cost 7.28%, against 6.34% a year earlier, tracking the 10-year yield more than the policy rate. What does a 25 bp hike change, and what can it do against energy-driven inflation? The article follows the chain down to credit, separating what is observed from what is estimated.

By Ludovic Labeaut10 min readState of knowledge as of 31 October 2026

01A central bank sets its own rates, not the market rate

A central bank does not set the rate at which banks lend to one another. It sets what it pays on their deposits and what it charges them when it lends to them. No bank will lend for less than it earns at the central bank: the market rate is bounded. This power rests on its monopoly over reserves, the money that banks hold in their accounts with it.

When reserves are scarce, the central bank adjusts their quantity every day to target the middle of a corridor between its two rates. When they are abundant, the market rate falls close to the rate paid on deposits, which becomes a floor. The Fed adopted this system on 30 January 2019. The ECB adjusted it on 13 March 2024: since 18 September 2024, its main refinancing operations (MRO) rate has been only 15 bp above the deposit facility, against 50 bp previously.

The same abundant-reserves regime therefore does not put the market rate in the same place: it all depends on who has access to the floor. Without access to IORB, US money market funds place their cash at the ON RRP; the EFFR sits 13 bp above it and stays close to IORB. The €STR is thought to include borrowing from non-bank lenders with no access to the deposit facility; this common explanation remains an interpretation. This step is fast: TONA reached 1.227% as early as 24 September, the date the hike took effect (BoJ).

The Fed operates almost entirely through securities. The Eurosystem still lends a little to banks (€29 bn), while nearly €2,300 bn remains deposited with it: excess liquidity remains massive. At the BoJ, the share of government bonds (JGBs) is the legacy of the purchases launched in 2013 and of yield curve control (2016–2024).

The Fed stopped reducing its balance sheet on 1 December 2025. Since 12 December, it has been buying Treasury bills for “reserve management”, about 40 billion dollars at the outset, purchases confirmed on 28 October 2026. This is not quantitative easing: these short-term securities keep reserves at a level deemed “ample” as banknotes in circulation and the Treasury's account grow (statement of 10 December 2025), without targeting long rates.

The Eurosystem has not reinvested the securities of its asset purchase programme (APP) since July 2023, nor those of its pandemic programme (PEPP) since late 2024: its securities holdings fell by €20 bn between 28 August and 25 September. The BoJ is slowing its JGB purchases, to about 2,100 bn yen a month planned for January–March 2027, then 2,000 bn yen (June 2026 plan). On 30 October, it raised its sales of exchange-traded funds (ETFs) from about 330 to 860 bn yen a year from January 2027.

02The structure of credit determines the speed

The ECB distinguishes six channels, without measuring their weight: the cost of credit and the return on savings; credit and balance sheets, when collateral loses value and banks tighten their lending supply (Bernanke and Gertler, 1995); asset prices and wealth; the exchange rate; risk-taking; and expectations, the only channel that acts directly on wages and prices.

US mortgage rates track the 10-year yield

In the United States, most mortgages are 30-year fixed-rate loans: a hike affects only new borrowers and those who move house. And their rate tracks the 10-year Treasury yield rather than the policy rate: it has risen by 94 bp in a year, in line with the 10-year. Monetary policy weighs on it first through expected short rates; article 6 of this series details the other component, the term premium.

The share of variable-rate loans (or loans fixed for one year at most) is well below its historical average, and close to zero in France (3.0% in August, same series): a French household that has just borrowed is now close to the US case. These shares relate to flows of new loans, not to the outstanding stock.

The euro area, financed mainly by banks, depends more on the credit channel. According to ECB Occasional Paper No. 389 (Altavilla et al., revised in August 2026), transmission to firms was “effective” in 2022–2023, with a peak effect on credit 8 to 12 months after a shock; variable-rate borrowers cut their investment by about 15 points more than the others. The study says nothing as clear-cut for households.

Lags that are estimated, not observed

Occasional Paper No. 393 (Ascari, Zlobins et al., July 2026) puts the lag before the full effect on GDP and headline inflation at 12 to 18 months, and at more than two years for underlying inflation and services; transmission is said to be faster when inflation is high. Adriana Kugler, a member of the Fed's Board of Governors, placed the peak effect after one to two years on 22 April 2025. These texts commit their authors, not their institution, and only their summaries were read for this article. And these lags are model estimates, not a rule for 2026.

Measuring the effect requires isolating the surprise

Central banks raise their rates when the economy overheats: the correlation between hikes and inflation reflects their reaction, not the effect of the hikes. The monetary policy shock has to be isolated: the part of a decision that the expected state of the economy does not explain.

Three methods attempt this: structural VAR models (systems of equations estimated on several series), which are sensitive to their assumptions (Christiano, Eichenbaum and Evans, 1999); narrative shocks, purged of the reaction to the Fed's internal forecasts (Romer and Romer, 2004); and high-frequency identification, which reads futures contracts in a narrow window around an announcement (Kuttner, 2001; Gertler and Karadi, 2015).

The last of these runs up against the information effect: a surprise hike may reveal that the Fed sees the economy as stronger (Nakamura and Steinsson, 2018), or reflect its reaction to public news not yet priced in by markets (Bauer and Swanson, 2023). The methods diverge (Ramey, 2016): no single figure commands a consensus.

03Against an energy shock, a hike acts on second-round effects

Inflation in 2026 is driven by energy. In the euro area, the harmonised index of consumer prices (HICP) rose by 3.8% year on year in September, with energy at +18.9% and the index excluding energy at +2.3% (Eurostat, 16 October).

Monetary policy does not bring down the price of oil. It acts on second-round effects: through expectations, to prevent wages from being indexed to prices; through demand, to limit the pass-through of costs; through the exchange rate, as a stronger euro lightens the dollar-denominated oil bill. The dilemma is explicit: on 29 October, the ECB saw energy as an upside risk to inflation and a downside risk to growth.

The exchange rate illustrates the limit. Between 1 July and 2 October, the euro fell by about 1.1% against the dollar despite the ECB's hike, and the dollar lost about 2.8% against the yen after the BoJ's hike (Fed H.10 series). These moves mix rate differentials, energy and risk aversion; without an identification method, they cannot be attributed to the decisions.

Three limits remain. The zero lower bound, not binding at current levels, forced central banks to rely on forward guidance and asset purchases (the Fed in 2009–2015, the BoJ until 2024). Fiscal policy can offset a tightening, or the reverse. Fragmentation, specific to the euro area, means that the same hike raises the cost of credit more where sovereign spreads widen: hence the Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT) of 2012 and the Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI) of 2022.

Precedent: “whatever it takes”, or the power of an announcement

In July 2012, the 10-year spread between Italy and Germany reached about 475 bp, after about 518 bp in November 2011 (calculation based on OECD monthly averages). On 26 July, Mario Draghi declared the ECB ready, within its mandate, to do “whatever it takes” to preserve the euro. On 6 September, it set out the details of OMT: purchases of government bonds with maturities of 1 to 3 years, conditional on a European assistance programme, with no quantitative limit set in advance.

OMT has never been used. Yet the spread had fallen back to about 324 bp by December 2012. Two monthly averages prove nothing, since other European decisions date from the same period. The event study by Altavilla, Giannone and Lenza (2016) isolates the announcements: a fall of about 2 percentage points in Italian and Spanish 2-year yields, with no comparable change in Germany and France.

On 21 July 2022, the day of its first hike since 2011, the ECB created the TPI on the same logic, so that the hike would be transmitted evenly. No activation has been found, without official confirmation at this stage.

04What remains uncertain

  • The “ample” level of reserves. Reserve demand is estimated, not observed, and it shifts. A New York Fed working paper (Afonso et al., Staff Report 1019, revised in November 2025) places saturation at around 12 to 13% of bank assets.
  • The strength of transmission. US fixed rates and the savings accumulated during the pandemic may have dampened the effect of the 2022–2023 hikes; other work points to rapid transmission to bank lending in the euro area. These readings rest on estimates and remain unsettled.
  • The €STR below the deposit facility: mere segmentation between banks and non-banks in one reading, imperfect transmission of the policy rate to part of the market in another.

05What to watch

  • 8–9 December 2026, the Fed's monetary policy committee (FOMC), with projections: a hike would move IORB and ON RRP, and the implementation note will show whether reserve management purchases change pace.
  • Every Thursday, the Fed's H.4.1 release: reserves (2,882 billion dollars on 30 September) and the spread between EFFR and IORB (−2 bp in late October). An EFFR moving up to IORB would signal less abundant reserves.
  • 16–17 December 2026, ECB, with projections; and the review of the operational framework parameters scheduled for 2026, with no decision published as of 7 October. It will show whether the structural operations and structural portfolio announced in 2024 are taking shape.
  • 17–18 December 2026, BoJ: the meeting will show whether the hike to 1.5% advocated by Hajime Takata in October wins over the Policy Board. January 2027 sees the start of the new pace of JGB purchases and of ETF sales at 860 bn yen a year, a first test of a smooth withdrawal.
