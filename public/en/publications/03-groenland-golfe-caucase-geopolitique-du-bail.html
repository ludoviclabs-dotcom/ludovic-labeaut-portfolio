Greenland, the Gulf, the Caucasus: the geopolitics of the lease

In the midst of the war against Iran, Washington is redrawing its alliances at three ends of the map. The common thread is neither conquest nor the classic alliance, but leasing: long-term usage rights, paid for by the host and backed by arms contracts and critical minerals.

By Ludovic Labeaut10 min readData as of 26 September 2026

Key points

  1. In five days, from 17 to 22 September, Washington notified Congress of the sale of 48 F-35s to Saudi Arabia ($24.3bn) and signed a defence agreement with no expiry date with Denmark and Greenland.
  2. The Caucasus has followed the same logic since the launch of the TRIPP corridor: a project company 74% owned by the United States for 49 years, running along the Iranian border.
  3. Arms sales have become a diplomatic currency: in the first quarter of 2026, the Middle East accounted for 81% of US foreign military sales.
  4. The paradox: the more transactional the guarantee, the more allies hedge. Witness the Mecca pact, Armenia’s turn towards the EU and the European military presence in Greenland.

01A doctrine: lease rather than conquer

The texts say so explicitly. The National Security Strategy of 4 December 2025 sets out a “Trump corollary” to the Monroe Doctrine and, rather than sharing the burden (burden-sharing), prefers to shift it wholesale onto allies (burden-shifting)1. The National Defense Strategy of 23 January 2026 ranks its priorities: the homeland, then China, the burden borne by allies and the industrial base. It designates the Panama Canal, the “Gulf of America” and Greenland as “key terrain”2.

Panama offers the mirror image. In late January, Panama’s Supreme Court declared CK Hutchison’s port concession (Balboa and Cristóbal, held since 1997) unconstitutional. Management was handed over on an interim basis to Maersk and MSC, and the Hong Kong group has launched arbitration3. There, a Chinese concession is revoked; elsewhere, it is Washington that obtains concessions. The agreements examined below share three features:

  • a long, even indefinite, term;
  • a cost passed on to the host;
  • screening of adversary capital and presence, Chinese first and foremost.

02Greenland: from crisis to agreement

The agreement of 22 September ends nearly two years of tension. In January 2026, Copenhagen and Nuuk were speaking of a “geopolitical crisis” and eleven European countries, France among them, sent reinforcements to the island. On 21 January, Donald Trump threatened eight European countries with tariffs, then announced that same evening in Davos, alongside NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte, a negotiating “framework” ruling out both force and tariffs4. France and Canada opened consulates in Nuuk on 6 February, NATO launched the Arctic Sentry mission on 11 February, and on 7 September Brussels announced a €200m package for Greenland5.

What the agreement contains

  • An old foundation. It amends the 1951 defence agreement. Pituffik base is modernised and two new defence areas are created, at Narsarsuaq (south) and Mestersvig (east), the latter facing the GIUK gap6.
  • Broader rights. US forces obtain overflight and landing rights, and access to territorial waters for their ships and submarines7.
  • Locks. No non-NATO state may establish a military presence without the consent of all three parties. Investors from outside NATO and the EU are excluded from controlling critical infrastructure and mineral extraction7.
  • No time limit. The agreement has no expiry date, and an independent Greenland would have to remain in NATO. The preamble reaffirms Danish sovereignty. Entry into force requires ratification by the Folketing (Danish parliament) and the Inatsisartut (Greenlandic parliament)68.
  • “No cost to the American taxpayer”, according to the White House9.

The verdict is disputed. For the French review Le Grand Continent, Europe’s strategy of presence paid off: there was no transfer of sovereignty10. Other analysts argue that most of these rights were already available under the 1951 regime11. The real American gain lies elsewhere. No rare-earth mine is in production in Greenland, and opening one will take “a decade or more”12. What Washington has secured, then, is not mines but a veto over capital. The date to watch is 10 November, when the suspension of Chinese export controls on rare earths expires13.

03The Gulf: the F-35 as currency

The notification of 17 September covers 48 F-35s and 49 F135 engines, worth $24.3bn. Congress has thirty days to review it, and deliveries are expected in the early 2030s14. It follows on from Mohammed bin Salman’s visit to Washington on 18 November 2025, which had produced four commitments15:

  • a strategic defence pact (the Strategic Defense Agreement), which is not a treaty ratified by the Senate but provides for Saudi funds to “defray” US costs;
  • major non-NATO ally status;
  • the promise of F-35s;
  • a framework on critical minerals.

Two breaks with precedent stand out. First, the sale is not conditional on Saudi–Israeli normalisation, contrary to what Israel wanted. Second, it unsettles the doctrine of qualitative military edge (QME), which guarantees Israel technological superiority over its neighbours: Israel had received 48 of its first 50 F-35s as of January 2026 and has 75 under contract16. The State Department maintains that the sale “will not alter the military balance” in the region; some lawmakers fear Chinese access to the technology17.

Yet the American guarantee is showing its limits. An executive order of 29 September 2025 made any attack on Qatar a threat to the security of the United States18, which did not stop Iran from striking Ras Laffan in March. The monarchies are therefore hedging. On 7 August 2026, Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan concluded a defence pact in Mecca under which an attack on one is an attack on all19. On 19 September, the Houthis struck Riyadh airport for the first time in four years13. And the same transactional logic is extending to Ankara, to which Donald Trump has promised the lifting of CAATSA sanctions and says he is “certainly” considering selling F-35s20.

04The Caucasus: a corridor under concession

On 8 August 2025, at the White House, Armenia and Azerbaijan signed a joint declaration and initialled a peace treaty, without signing or ratifying it. Baku is demanding, as a precondition, an amendment to Armenia’s constitution21. The same day saw the birth of the “Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity” (TRIPP): 42 to 43 km of rail, road, energy networks and fibre along the Aras river, which marks the border with Iran22.

The arrangement is a lease in the literal sense. The TRIPP development company is 74% owned by the United States and 26% by Armenia for 49 years, with a possible 50-year extension that would raise Armenia’s share to 49%23. On 26 May 2026, in Yerevan, Secretary of State Marco Rubio initialled the framework agreement, a strategic partnership charter and a memorandum of understanding on critical minerals (copper, molybdenum, gold)24. The obstacles remain real:

  • in the parliamentary elections of 7 June, Nikol Pashinyan’s party kept its majority (49.9%) but lost the two-thirds needed to amend the constitution25;
  • Moscow is imposing embargo after embargo (mineral water, wine, cognac) and threatening Armenia’s continued membership of the Eurasian Economic Union, with Russia accounting for around 30% of its trade26;
  • Iran accepted TRIPP on 21 September, on condition that no US military presence is established near its border27.

06What this changes for Europe and France

Presence buys influence. In Greenland, Europe’s show of resolve in January (troops, a French consulate in Nuuk, an EU funding package) contributed to an outcome that preserves Danish sovereignty. Investment screening excludes capital from outside NATO and the EU, but leaves European capital eligible. This is a window for a European critical minerals policy, provided it is seized before the end of the decade.

Legitimacy is not ownership. In the Caucasus, Europe supplies the political framework (the European Political Community summit in Yerevan in May, the prospect of an EU membership bid announced by Nikol Pashinyan)26, but the United States owns the asset.

The burden is shifting to Europe. At the Ankara summit (July 2026), five allies were above 3.5% of GDP in “core” defence spending, compared with 2.22% for France and 3.17% for the United States28. And France is now militarily engaged in the Gulf, with the deployment of assets to Yanbu announced on 25 September. The logic of “burden-shifting” is not a distant threat: it is already under way.
