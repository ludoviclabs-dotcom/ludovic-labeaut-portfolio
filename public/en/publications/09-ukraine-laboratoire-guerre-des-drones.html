Ukraine, an open-air laboratory for drone warfare

In three years, Ukraine has gone from a few thousand drones to several million a year. Along the way, it has invented an air defence costing a few thousand dollars per shot. The Pentagon now comes there for lessons, technologies and, increasingly, suppliers.

By Ludovic Labeaut10 min readData as of 26 September 2026

Key points

  1. A step change in scale. Ukraine produced 2.2 million drones in 2024 and more than 4 million in 2025, and is aiming for over 7 million in 2026. According to RUSI, drones were already responsible for 60–70% of Russian equipment destroyed in early 2025.
  2. The decisive innovation is defensive. Interceptor drones costing $1,000–2,500 are shooting down Russian Shaheds: in February 2026, they accounted for more than 70% of interceptions over Kyiv.
  3. The Pentagon is buying the method more than the product: competitive trials, mandatory joint ventures, combat data to train its AI models. The big drone deal between Washington and Kyiv has still not been signed.
  4. Lasers move into production: $464.8m for the US Army’s LOCUST X3, at under $5 a shot. The deeper lesson lies elsewhere: defences must be layered (laser, gun, interceptor drone, missile) according to the cost of the threat.

01A war industry counted in millions

In three years, Ukraine has built up an industrial base that Western armed forces take a decade to design. Several milestones contributed to this:

  • the Army of Drones programme, launched in July 20221;
  • a fully fledged service branch, the Unmanned Systems Forces, established in 20242;
  • Brave1 Market, a marketplace where units order directly from more than 2,600 products made by over 600 manufacturers3.

The most original mechanism is the “e-points” system. Each confirmed kill earns points: 12 for an infantryman, 40 for a tank, up to 50 for a multiple rocket launcher. Units then convert them into equipment, delivered in ten days on average3. Military procurement thus becomes a feedback loop.

Production has followed suit: 2.2 million drones in 2024, more than 4 million in 20254 and a target of over 7 million in 2026, against a declared capacity of more than 8 million a year5.

At the front, the effect is massive, even though the scope of the figures varies. According to RUSI, tactical drones accounted for 60–70% of Russian equipment destroyed or damaged in early 20256. For 2025, the Ukrainian ministry puts the figure at four targets in five7. The technology is evolving month by month:

  • Fibre optics. Fibre-optic drones, which are immune to jamming, have a range of 10–20 km8.
  • AI terminal guidance. Modules costing around $100 have raised the hit rate from 20% to 80% in one brigade (single source)9.
  • Deep strike. The FP-1 drone costs around $55,000 for a range of 1,600 km; the Flamingo missile reaches 3,000 km10. On 20 September, more than 1,000 Ukrainian drones were launched in a single night, 450 of them towards Moscow11.

02The $2,000 air defence

The most exportable breakthrough is defensive. Against Russian Shahed and Geran drones, Ukraine has developed mass-produced interceptor drones: 100,000 units in 202512. Wild Hornets’ Sting costs around $2,100–2,500 and the P1-SUN around $1,00013. In February 2026, these machines accounted for more than 70% of Shahed interceptions over Kyiv14. That amounts to a cost ratio of roughly 1 to 10 in the defender’s favour: the exact reverse of the PAC-3-versus-Shahed equation described in Note No. 7.

The race goes on. Russian launches peaked at around 5,200 Shahed and Geran drones in May 202615. Jet-powered versions, flying at 400–600 km/h, require faster interceptors: according to Ukrainian operators, interceptor technology becomes obsolete every six weeks on average13.

03Lasers, guns, microwaves: the American response

Lasers go into production. On 2 September, the US Army awarded AeroVironment a $464.8m contract for several dozen LOCUST X3 lasers of around 30 kW (E-HEL programme). It is the first production contract for a military laser in American history, at an announced cost of less than $5 per shot16. The limitations are well known: weather, the dwell time required on each target, and airspace incidents during trials on the Mexican border in early 202617. Elsewhere, Britain’s DragonFire (£316m) is due to equip Type 45 destroyers in 2027, at around £10 a shot18. Israel’s Iron Beam was delivered at the end of 2025, but its combat use remains disputed19.

The return of the gun. On 4 September, the US Air Force issued a request for information on 20–40 mm anti-aircraft guns to protect its bases against drones and subsonic cruise missiles. Responses are due on 9 October20. With a 20 mm shell costing around $81, a two-second burst comes to roughly $12,000. Germany’s Gepard has demonstrated as much in Ukraine: anti-aircraft artillery remains relevant.

A dedicated organisation. Set up in August 2025, JIATF-401 leads the Pentagon’s counter-drone effort and can commit up to $50m per initiative21. In May 2026, a $500m framework agreement was awarded to Perennial Autonomy for interceptors proven in Ukraine, including the Merops, credited with more than 4,000 Russian drones destroyed22. The war against Iran served as a full-scale test: by 1st April, the UAE had been targeted by 2,012 drones, 438 ballistic missiles and 19 cruise missiles23.

04From laboratory to supplier

The relationship has been reversed, but slowly. In July 2025, Volodymyr Zelensky announced that the United States would buy $10–30bn worth of Ukrainian drones24. By March 2026, the Ukrainian offer had reached $35–50bn in equally owned joint ventures25. Then:

  • 8 July 2026. Donald Trump, previously reluctant, says he is ready to buy26.
  • 22 July. Six Ukrainian companies are authorised to export around a hundred drones each to the United States, and a declaration of intent on co-production is signed27.
  • 28 July and 22 September. Two meetings between the presidents, but no signature on the comprehensive agreement28.

The Pentagon prefers a controlled learning process. In the Gauntlet II competition (August 2026), around 60,000 drones were shared out among ten companies. The four Ukrainian firms did not make the top five, and the order is conditional on setting up an American joint venture29. On 21 September, the US Army signed a $10.64m contract for Skyeton’s Raybird drone, presented as the first major export of Ukrainian military drones to the United States30.

The real transfer is of data. Since January 2026, the Brave1 Dataroom platform has made it possible to train Palantir AI models on Ukrainian combat data31. On 1st September, Mykhailo Fedorov, formerly Minister of Digital Transformation and then of Defence, launched his own defence company. His first investor is Alex Karp, Palantir’s CEO, in a personal capacity rather than through the company32. Ukrainian lessons are already feeding into the American arsenal: the LUCAS drone is derived from a captured Shahed, and two of the FAMM missiles descend from the ERAM supplied to Kyiv (see Note No. 7).

“Washington is buying not so much Ukrainian drones as a lessons-learned feedback loop.”
The view from Europe & France

The “drone wall” has yet to be built

  • The September 2025 shock. Between nineteen and twenty-three Russian drones entered Poland. NATO responded with F-35s firing AIM-120 missiles at around $1m apiece against cheap drones: the cost equation had reached Europe33.
  • The European Drone Defence Initiative (EDDI). Stemming from the “Readiness 2030” roadmap, it aims for initial capability by the end of 2026 and full capability by the end of 202734. The drone coalition led by Latvia and the United Kingdom brings together twenty countries35.
  • France. The Drone Pact brings together more than 170 manufacturers; Harmattan AI delivered 1,000 drones at under €1,000 apiece in less than a year36. Cilas’s HELMA-P laser protected the 2024 Olympic Games, and three more units have been ordered37.

Europe has adopted the Ukrainian grammar (interceptors, lasers, cheap drones), but is still at the stage of pilot orders. The ability to produce in the hundreds of thousands has yet to be demonstrated.

05The limits of the model

  • Low effectiveness per drone. Between 60% and 80% of FPV drones miss their target, and both electronic warfare and the weather degrade their use6.
  • Artillery is not dead. Jamming, weather or a severed fibre: some missions remain the preserve of the gun alone38.
  • Dependence on China. Motors, batteries and fibre: “drones win battles, components win wars”, as RUSI sums it up39.
  • A model that is hard to transpose. Replicator delivered hundreds of drones rather than thousands. The requirement for 100% American components is holding back production. And buying drones achieves nothing without doctrine, training and lessons-learned feedback loops40.
  • Unverifiable figures. The reported results of operations such as Spiderweb (1st June 2025), or of Iron Beam, are claims, not proof.
