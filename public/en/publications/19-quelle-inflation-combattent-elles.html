Which Inflation Are They Fighting? One “2%”, Three Indices, an Energy Shock

In September 2026, inflation picked up, driven by energy. In the United States, the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index rose 3.6% year on year, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis (29 October). In the euro area, the harmonised index rose 3.8%, with energy up 18.9%, according to Eurostat (16 October). In Japan, the national index rose 2.1%, according to the Statistics Bureau (23 October). All three central banks aim for 2%. But that figure does not mean the same thing at the Fed, the ECB and the Bank of Japan (BoJ): coverage, the weight of housing and formulas differ. This article explains how to read these indices, where inflation comes from, why it persists and why Japan faces the opposite problem.

By Ludovic Labeaut10 min readState of knowledge as of 31 October 2026

01The same “2%” does not measure the same thing in Washington, Frankfurt and Tokyo

The Fed targets 2% as measured by the PCE, calculated by the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) (22 August 2025 strategy). The ECB targets 2%, symmetrically and over the medium term, as measured by the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP) (30 June 2025 strategy). The BoJ targets 2% as measured by the national consumer price index (CPI) (22 January 2013 target). The table compares the three indices on what makes their figures diverge.

Owner-occupied housing is the biggest difference (methods: BEA, Eurostat, Statistics Bureau; figures published on 29, 16 and 23 October). In December 2025, housing accounted for 35.6% of the US CPI, including 26.2% for owners' equivalent rent (BLS). The ECB tracks it separately.

PCE versus CPI, French CPI versus HICP

The BEA breaks down the gap between its index and the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) CPI into formula, weight and scope effects, plus a residual (FAQ 555). Over 2002–2007, the CPI rose by about 0.4 percentage point a year more than the PCE: the formula explained about half of the gap, the weight of housing the rest, with health care working in the opposite direction (McCully, Moyer and Stewart, 2007). This is one of the reasons put forward for targeting the PCE, which is considered more comprehensive.

In 2026, the gap reversed for core inflation, excluding food and energy: 3.0% for the PCE against 2.4% for the CPI in August (BEA, BLS), then 3.1% against 2.6% in September (BEA, BLS). As the BEA's reconciliation table could not be found on 8 October, the explanation remains an interpretation. By deduction, housing is not the cause: it rose 3.0% in the CPI in August, more than core, and weighs less in the PCE. A note from an asset manager points to items that the CPI ignores or weights lightly, which have risen sharply since January: software, portfolio management, legal services (ANIMA, 28 August 2026, unquantified commentary). Yet the price of portfolio management follows the stock market, not pressure on consumer prices.

In France, the consumer price index (INSEE's IPC) rose 3.0% and the HICP 3.4% in September 2026, according to the provisional estimate (INSEE, 30 Sept.). The HICP measures health net of reimbursements, includes non-market education and health and, since 2026, gambling (methodological note); the share of each item has not been established. Only the HICP is used to assess the ECB's target.

Core, alternative measures and timing

Core inflation removes volatile items, but not the same ones everywhere: food and energy in the United States; energy, food, alcohol and tobacco in the euro area. In Japan, the widely cited “core” removes only fresh food and keeps energy; the “core-core” removes both. In August 2026, the first stood at 1.7%, the second at 1.9% (Statistics Bureau, 18 Sept.).

Other measures strip out extreme changes each month: the trimmed mean PCE of the Dallas Fed, the median CPI of the Cleveland Fed. In August, they stood between 2.2% and 2.6%, a sign that the rise was concentrated in a few items. In the euro area, the range of measures tracked by the ECB ran from 2.9% to 6.9% in June 2023 (Bańbura et al., 2023). None dominates in all circumstances, and removing energy can mask its spread to other prices when the shock lasts.

Finally, each figure has several lives. The HICP first appears as a flash estimate at the end of the month, then as final data around the middle of the following month: September energy went from 18.8% (2 Oct.) to 18.9% (16 Oct.). The unadjusted CPI is final on publication, but its seasonally adjusted version is revised back five years every February (BLS).

Tool: year-on-year rate, annualised rate, base effect

The year-on-year rate compares a month with the same month of the previous year. The annualised rate scales a short-period change up to a full year; it requires a seasonally adjusted series.

Using the PCE price index (vintage of 30 September 2026, FRED, extracted on 7 October), the calculation gives, for August 2026, 3.42% year on year (131.579 / 127.229 − 1), 3.57% at an annualised rate over six months and 1.03% over three months, dragged down by a fall in the index in June.

The base effect: in September 2025, the index had risen by 0.29%. With that month dropping out of the calculation, a 0.2% rise in September 2026 would have brought the year-on-year rate down to 3.32%, without any fall in prices. The BEA ultimately published 3.6% on 29 October.

Vintages, that is, the state of a series at a given publication date: the annual update of the accounts on 30 September 2026 revised the index back to January 2021 (BEA). The June-to-July rise, annualised, falls from 1.89% to 0.61% depending on the vintage; mixing the two produces a fictitious fall. The attribution of the earlier levels to the 26 August vintage is inferred from the ALFRED page, not verified by download.

02An energy shock only becomes lasting through second-round effects

Most recent work starts from the expectations-augmented Phillips curve: inflation equals expected inflation, plus an effect of labour market tightness, plus cost shocks. The slope of the curve measures how sensitive inflation is to this tightness, judged by the gap between unemployment and its equilibrium level, which is estimated, not observed. Excess demand, supply shocks and margins only make inflation last if they pass through to wages and expectations.

As long as the shock remains a relative price, its effect on inflation fades. Faced with a supply shock, a rate hike does not target the price of energy, but second-round effects.

2021–2023: three competing readings

The US CPI peaked at 9.1% year on year in June 2022, in unadjusted data (BLS, 13 July 2022). The HICP peaked at 10.6% in October 2022 (10.7% in the flash estimate; Eurostat, 17 November 2022). Three readings, more complementary than mutually exclusive, dominate.

For Bernanke and Blanchard (2025), a peer-reviewed article, the US rise came mainly from shocks to energy, food and shortages; labour market tightness weighed less at the time, but lasted longer. Their working paper on eleven economies (2024) adds that the shocks did not become entrenched, partly thanks to credible targets. Ball, Leigh and Mishra (2022, Brookings Papers) emphasise the pass-through of shocks to core inflation, which also reflects the tightness measured by the ratio of job vacancies to unemployed workers. For the euro area, Hansen, Toscani and Zhou (2023), in a working paper that does not represent the views of the IMF, attribute about 40% of the rise in the consumption deflator between the first quarters of 2022 and 2023 to import prices, and 45% to domestic profits, without a generalised rise in margins.

These model estimates settle neither the role of fiscal policy nor the question of a reaction that came too late, argued by Reis (2022) in a Bank for International Settlements working paper that reflects only his own views: in his account, central banks underestimated the risk of de-anchoring.

Why inflation persists

Hazell, Herreño, Nakamura and Steinsson (2022) estimate a flat slope using US state data: reducing inflation through slower activity alone is costly in unemployment, and the disinflation of the 1980s would have been driven mainly by expectations. Benigno and Eggertsson, in a working paper not published in a journal, argue that the slope steepens when the labour market is very tight, which would explain the rapid rise of 2021–2022 and then disinflation without recession; the hypothesis remains debated. Finally, firms' expectations, studied in New Zealand, are dispersed and poorly informed (Coibion, Gorodnichenko and Kumar, 2018).

In an example with textbook values (expectations at 2.5%, a slope of 0.2, unemployment half a percentage point below equilibrium), bringing inflation down from 2.6% to 2% with unchanged expectations would require 3 percentage points more unemployment; if expectations return to 2%, it is enough for unemployment to return to its equilibrium level. With a flat slope, credibility does most of the work: a credible central bank can look through a supply shock, one that is not must react more strongly. Any estimated slope remains very imprecise (Mavroeidis, Plagborg-Møller and Stock, 2014).

The first round dominates in Europe; in the United States, core inflation shows that the problem is not just energy (sources: Eurostat, BEA, BLS, Statistics Bureau). In the euro area, headline inflation exceeds inflation excluding energy by 1.5 percentage points (calculation: 3.8 − 2.3); AFP links the shock to the resumption of hostilities in the Middle East in early July (3 Oct. 2026, press, causality not established). The rise in services inflation, from 3.0% in August to 3.2% in September (flash estimate), may still reflect indirect effects, and no published decomposition covers this shock. 03Japan has long fought the opposite problem

From the late 1990s to the early 2010s, Japan experienced deflation or inflation close to zero. The mechanism is persistence in reverse: expectations anchored near zero and sluggish wages hold back any pick-up. According to a BoJ working paper that does not represent the views of the institution, the zero lower bound on interest rates and subdued expectations prevented 2% from being reached on a lasting basis, and the price–wage cycle strengthened after the pandemic (Fukunaga, Hogen and Ueno, 2024).

After the adoption of the 2% target in January 2013, quantitative and qualitative easing (April 2013), the negative interest rate (January 2016) and yield curve control (September 2016) were aimed at shifting these expectations. According to De Michelis and Iacoviello (2016), raising a target has limited effects when its credibility is low. Using its own model, the BoJ estimates the effect of these policies at 0.5–0.7 percentage point of inflation (December 2024 review). These measures ended on 19 March 2024 (BoJ).

In 2026, the debate is about measurement. On 18 September, the BoJ wrote that underlying inflation “has been approaching 2 percent” (statement); its two dissenters were looking at the index excluding fresh food, still at 1.7% in August. In September, the headline index (2.1%) remained below the measure excluding fresh food and energy (2.4%): taken together, these two items are rising less than the rest. On 30 October, the Outlook report judged underlying inflation “broadly consistent” with 2% and projected, as the median of Policy Board members, 2.5%, 2.6% and then 2.0% excluding fresh food for fiscal years 2026 to 2028 (Outlook); one member, Hajime Takata, considered the target already achieved (statement). In Japan, the question is no longer how to exit deflation, but whether inflation close to 2% is becoming embedded in wages.

04What remains uncertain

  • The reversal of the core gap between the PCE and the CPI remains unexplained until the BEA's reconciliation table has been read. The financial-items lead rests on unquantified commentary, which itself expects the gap to narrow after methodological revisions.
  • The slope of the Phillips curve: flat and stable, or steeper when the labour market is tight? The answer changes the cost of disinflation in 2026.
  • Second-round effects in the euro area: one month of rising services inflation does not prove them; negotiated wages and expectations have to be read over several months.
  • Without long series and the BoJ's and ECB's trend measures, the diagnosis rests on the latest published data points.

05What to watch

  • The October HICP flash estimate, not yet published as of 2 November: is energy still pushing the index up, and does services inflation exceed 3.2%?
  • The October CPI, then PCE: does the PCE's 0.5 percentage point lead on core inflation hold?
  • The meetings of 8–9 December (Fed, with projections), 16–17 December (ECB, with Eurosystem projections) and 17–18 December (BoJ): each will show how it separates the first round from the second.
  • The next BoJ Outlook, in January 2027, then the spring wage negotiations: will the 2.6% projection for fiscal 2027 hold, and the price–wage cycle with it?
