A dependent Europe: what France can really control

A dependency becomes strategic when a supplier can interrupt, raise the price of, or attach conditions to a capability that is hard to replace. Energy, defence, digital: Europe is not exposed in the same way in these three areas. France has real strengths, but its room for manoeuvre also depends on choices made elsewhere.

By Ludovic Labeaut4 min readData as of 26 September 2026

MacroInsider’s analyses shed light on two levers of American power: sanctions, and the capital mobilised to rebuild strategic industry, notably missile production capacity. [1] For France and Europe, the question becomes: which capabilities would be hard to replace in a prolonged crisis?

France’s CEPII research centre notes that in 2023, of the imported products for which the EU had a concentrated dependency, only 20, or 5%, came mainly from the United States. This indicator covers categories of goods; it measures neither military dependency, nor digital services, nor energy. Vulnerability has to be described sector by sector. [2]

01Energy: French power, imported fuels

In 2024, net imports covered 57% of the energy available in the EU, according to Eurostat. In 2025, the United States supplied 56% of the liquefied natural gas imported by the EU: this share refers to LNG, not to all the gas consumed in Europe. Replacing one supplier without diversifying the others can shift the concentration risk. [3]

RTE, France’s grid operator, reports a French net electricity export balance of 92 TWh in 2025. Yet fossil fuels still account for nearly 60% of total energy consumed. In 2024, France imported its gas from Norway (40%), the United States (21%), Russia (18%) and Algeria (11%). The electricity advantage does not erase the vulnerability of uses that still depend on fossil fuels. [4]

02Defence: spending more, having the means

The 27 member states spent €418bn on defence in 2025, according to the European Defence Agency. Joint procurement accounted for 24% of equipment spending. Raising budgets does not instantly create interoperable systems and factories able to deliver quickly. [5]

The ECFR identifies capabilities that remain largely dependent on the United States: command, space surveillance, theatre logistics, advanced communications. The European Commission lists strategic enablers, air and missile defence and military mobility among the gaps to be filled. They are what allow national forces to operate together. [6]

France has a nuclear deterrent and a substantial defence industry. These strengths do not meet the collective needs of a large-scale European operation. The American case followed by MacroInsider is also a reminder that an order requires supply chains and manufacturing capacity. [1][6]

03Technology: who controls the invisible layers?

A French government body can host its data in Europe while using foreign software, cloud or computing power. Ifri (the French Institute of International Relations) stresses that data localisation does not resolve questions relating to the provider and to the extraterritorial laws that may apply to it. The institute also describes dependencies in semiconductors and networks. [7]

Vulnerability emerges when an essential service has to be kept running or moved quickly: can one switch operator, update the software and access computing power? Ifri recommends improving service continuity and diversifying partnerships. A fallback has to be operational. [7]

04The French test: replace, produce, protect

The economic security doctrine published by Bercy (France’s economy ministry) on 24 September 2026 targets critical industrial, scientific and technological assets. Among other things, it provides for foreign investment screening and support through grants or equity, in an economy that remains open and with proportionate responses. [8]

Three questions help to assess a dependency: who supplies the capability, how long would it take to replace them, and what happens during the interruption? For France, they point to reducing fossil-fuel use, investing in shared military capabilities and demanding verifiable continuity of critical digital services. Room for manoeuvre is measured by the ability to keep acting when a relationship comes under strain.
