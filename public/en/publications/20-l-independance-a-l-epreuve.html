Independence Under Strain: What Grounds It, What Protects It, What Weakens It

On 28 October 2026, Governor Lisa Cook voted for a rate hike that the majority of the Federal Reserve’s monetary policy committee voted down. On 29 June, the Supreme Court had rejected, by five votes to four, the government’s application in the proceedings arising from the attempt to remove her. Kevin Warsh has chaired the Fed since 22 May; the European Central Bank announced on 29 October the early departure of Christine Lagarde. These facts say nothing about anyone’s intentions. They raise a question: what grounds a central bank’s independence, what protects it, and where can it wear thin? The article revisits the theoretical argument, compares the three legal frameworks and separates what 2026 establishes from what remains to be confirmed.

By Ludovic Labeaut11 min readState of knowledge as of 31 October 2026

01In 2026, the written rules were tested by events

Kevin Warsh has chaired the Fed’s Board of Governors and the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), which votes on rates, since 22 May 2026 (Fed). Jerome Powell, Chair from 2018 to May 2026, remains a member of the Board, and therefore a voter.

In August 2025, the President of the United States attempted to remove Governor Lisa Cook. On 29 June 2026, the Supreme Court rejected the government’s application by five votes to four (Trump v. Cook, No. 25A312, Supreme Court); Lisa Cook remains in office while the proceedings continue. The text of the opinion was not read for this article: its scope as summarised by a law firm, judicial review of the grounds for removal, remains to be confirmed. The outcome of the application is known; the Court’s reasoning is not yet.

On 28 October, the FOMC held its target range at 3.75–4.00% by ten votes to two (statement). Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, President of the Cleveland Fed, wanted a 25 basis point (bp) hike, i.e. 0.25 percentage point. This vote says nothing about motives; it establishes that a governor whose removal was attempted sits and votes on the record. Article 1 of this series details these decisions.

On 29 October 2026, the ECB announced that Christine Lagarde will leave office on 7 February 2027, although her eight-year term ran until 31 October 2027 (statement). The text gives no reason; this article assumes none. Her successor will be appointed by the European Council, under Article 283 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU). At the Bank of Japan (BoJ), Kazuo Ueda has been Governor since 9 April 2023, with a term running until 8 April 2028.

A judicial test, a succession, a continuity: none of these three cases allows intentions to be read. The rule followed here is to track appointments and court decisions without prejudging motives.

02Independence answers a commitment problem, and rests on a correlation

The founding argument is time inconsistency: the temptation, for an authority, to renege on a promise once others have acted in reliance on it (Kydland and Prescott, 1977; Barro and Gordon, 1983). An authority that promises low inflation has an incentive, once wages and prices are set, to create a little surprise inflation to support activity. Households and firms anticipate this; inflation ends up higher, with no gain in activity. The problem presupposes no bad faith: it lies in the promise itself.

Two responses have been proposed. The first entrusts monetary policy to an official more “conservative” about inflation than society is (Rogoff, 1985), at the cost of more variable activity during supply shocks, those that push prices up while weighing on output. This is the type of shock that energy imposes on the three banks in 2026 (article 2 of this series).

The second distinguishes two kinds of independence (Debelle and Fischer, 1994). Goal independence means choosing one’s own target; instrument independence means choosing only the means of reaching a given target. The authors prefer the second, under a mandate set by law: the legislature says what, the central bank says how.

That leaves the evidence. Across 16 OECD countries, greater legal independence goes hand in hand with lower inflation, with no visible net effect on growth (Alesina and Summers, 1993). On a global dataset from 1970 to 2012, this link depends on the choice of countries and periods (Garriga, 2016). It can also be read the other way round: countries that abhor inflation may choose independent banks. Independence rests on a sound theoretical argument and on a statistical regularity, not on proof of causality.

Precedent: Volcker, 1979–1982, the price of credibility

In September 1979, the federal funds rate, at which US banks lend each other reserves overnight, averaged 11.43% (FRED, FEDFUNDS series). At the time, the Fed had neither a numerical inflation target nor a statement on the day of its decisions. On Saturday 6 October 1979, it raised its discount rate from 11% to 12% and placed more emphasis on the supply of bank reserves, less on the stability of the rate (New York Fed Circular No. 8653): it changed instrument, not just level.

The rate reached 17.61% in April 1980, peaked at 19.10% in June 1981 and fell back to 8.95% in December 1982. Twelve-month inflation, calculated on the consumer price index not seasonally adjusted, went from a peak of 14.8% in March 1980 to 3.8% in December 1982. Unemployment reached 10.8% in November and December 1982 (FRED, UNRATE series).

Goodfriend and King (2005) describe this disinflation as “incredible”: lacking initial credibility, it would have cost more than with a central bank taken at its word. This is a model-based interpretation; their inflation figures, moreover, rely on other measures. The 1979 framework is not that of 2026; the case shows only that credibility is built before the shock and that, on this reading, its absence is paid for in jobs.

03Three architectures for the same 2% target

In the United States, section 2A of the Federal Reserve Act, stemming from a law of 16 November 1977, charges the Fed with promoting maximum employment, stable prices and moderate long-term interest rates. The first two objectives form the dual mandate: two goals that the law does not rank. The 2% figure, measured on the personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, is the FOMC’s choice, formalised in January 2012.

In the euro area, Article 127 of the TFEU makes price stability the primary objective; support for the Union’s economic policies comes only “without prejudice” to it. The mandate is hierarchical. Article 130 prohibits the ECB and the national central banks from seeking or taking instructions. The Treaty does not quantify price stability: the ECB defines it itself, since 2021 as 2% over the medium term on the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP), a symmetric target where a downward deviation is as undesirable as an upward one.

In Japan, the 1997 Bank of Japan Act (unofficial translation) sets price stability as the objective (Art. 2) and recognises the bank’s autonomy (Art. 3). It also requires the bank to maintain close contact with the government, monetary policy being “a component of overall economic policy” (Art. 4). The Minister of Finance and the minister in charge of economic policy may attend meetings and request that a vote be postponed to the next meeting; the Policy Board decides whether to accept (Art. 19). The text combines autonomy and consistency, but opens a formal channel to the government that exists neither at the Fed nor at the ECB. The sources consulted do not say whether this right has ever been used. The 2% target on the consumer price index (CPI) dates from 22 January 2013, adopted with a joint statement by the government and the BoJ.

In all three cases, Debelle and Fischer’s boundary is less clear-cut than in theory: the law sets the objective in words, the bank puts a number on it. And a mandate says what to aim for, not how to weigh risks at a given date.

The 2025 strategies: same target, different reaction

The Fed revised its strategy on 22 August 2025, a text reaffirmed on 27 January 2026. Two formulations from the August 2020 version disappear: flexible average inflation targeting, which aimed for 2% “on average over time” by accepting an overshoot after a period of inflation running too low, and the reaction only to employment shortfalls. The text does not name what it drops; Jerome Powell’s speech the same day explains it.

The ECB’s strategy statement of 30 June 2025 keeps the symmetric target adopted in July 2021. But the “forceful or persistent” response, which the 2021 version reserved for situations where rates are close to their lower bound, now extends to large and sustained deviations in both directions.

The 2% target has changed at none of the three banks; what has changed is the way they respond to it. At the BoJ, it is the instruments that have moved: on 19 March 2024, the negative interest rate and yield curve control came to an end, deemed to have “fulfilled their roles”. Each bank thus exposes itself to being judged against a public, dated text.

What is published, and to whom each answers

The FOMC has twelve voting members: the seven members of the Board, the President of the New York Fed and four regional Fed presidents on an annual rotation (Fed). The ECB’s Governing Council has 27 members but 21 votes: the six members of the Executive Board and 15 voting rights rotating each month among 21 national governors (ECB). The nine members of the BoJ’s Policy Board vote (Art. 16). The Fed and the BoJ publish named votes, dissents included, with the decision; the ECB publishes an account about four weeks later, without named votes.

October 2026 shows what this difference produces. We know that Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack wanted to raise rates, and that at the BoJ, which held its rate by eight votes to one on 30 October, Hajime Takata proposed 1.5% (statement). From the ECB, all we have is the President’s word: a “unanimous” decision after a discussion of a hike (press conference of 29 Oct.). Publication makes members individually accountable; non-publication can shield governors from national pressures. These are two arguments; no empirical result used here settles the matter.

Accountability, the obligation to answer for one’s actions, follows three models. The Fed Chair testifies before Congress twice a year, around 20 February and 20 July (section 2B). The ECB President appears before the European Parliament’s ECON committee every quarter (ECB). The BoJ submits a written report to the Diet roughly every six months, through the Minister of Finance (Art. 54).

04What remains uncertain

  • The scope of Trump v. Cook: the rejection of 29 June 2026 is established; the Court’s reasoning will only be known once the opinion is read, and the proceedings continue.
  • The causal effect of independence: the link with low inflation is a correlation, sensitive to the sample, possibly reversed. The sources used offer no causal identification.
  • The gap between legal and actual independence: the texts protect (Article 130 TFEU, the decision of 29 June), but pressure can come through appointments, public statements or the threat of legislation. Nothing in the sources consulted allows any intention to be attributed to the actors of 2026.
  • The scope of the 2025 revisions: one reading sees a return to a more symmetric reaction after the 2021–2023 inflation, another a change of vocabulary, with target and instruments unchanged. It is too early to tell.

05What to watch

  • 8–9 December 2026, FOMC: named votes and participants’ projections (Summary of Economic Projections). As the last meeting of the 2026 rotation, it will show whether Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack’s position remains in the minority.
  • 16–17 December 2026, ECB: the last meeting of the year under Christine Lagarde’s presidency; its account, about four weeks later, will show the range of positions, without votes.
  • 17–18 December 2026, BoJ: the recorded vote will show whether Hajime Takata’s dissent remains isolated.
  • Christine Lagarde’s successor, appointed by the European Council (Article 283 TFEU): the departure is set for 7 February 2027; the timetable for the appointment was not known as of 31 October. Part of actual independence is played out through this channel.
  • Trump v. Cook and the February hearing: the opinion and the rest of the proceedings, with no date known as of 31 October, will settle the scope of the protection. The law provides for a hearing before Congress around 20 February 2027; for 2026, only the July report appears on the Fed’s website.
