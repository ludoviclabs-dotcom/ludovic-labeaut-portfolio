From chatbot to co-scientist: how AI discovery systems work

In May 2026, Nature published two systems presented as “co-scientists”: Co-Scientist, from Google, and Robin, from the non-profit organisation FutureHouse. Both proposed therapeutic leads that were then tested on cells. These results are real, but they do not show that a machine does science on its own. They describe an organisation: several software agents that propose, critique, rank and revise hypotheses, connected to databases and overseen by researchers. Understanding these mechanics makes it possible to see where the added value, the blind spots and the risks lie.

By Ludovic Labeaut8 min readState of knowledge as of 8 October 2026

01The question

On its own, a large language model predicts plausible text. A scientific hypothesis must be new, consistent with the data, testable, and ultimately confirmed by experiment. How do we get from one to the other? And above all: at what point in the cycle does an independent check come in?

02The technology, explained

Five building blocks

Building block Simple definition Role in discovery
Reasoning model Language model trained to break a problem down into steps before answering Formulating hypotheses and arguing for them
Tools Functions the model can call: literature search, databases (ChEMBL, UniProt), code, simulators, AlphaFold Grounding reasoning in verifiable data
Agent Program that chains actions (searching, calculating, writing) towards a goal, with memory Carrying out a long task without intervention at every step
Multi-agent system Several agents with different roles that communicate with one another Separating production from critique, as in a team
Verifier Mechanism that judges a result: proof assistant, test, physical measurement, human expert Telling the plausible from the true

The example of Co-Scientist

According to the Nature paper and the Google DeepMind blog (19 May 2026), the system is built on Gemini and comprises:

  • a supervisor agent, which plans and distributes the work;
  • Generation, which proposes hypotheses;
  • Reflection, which critiques them, with access to external search;
  • Ranking, which runs a tournament of pairwise comparisons and simulated debates, scored with an Elo system (the chess rating system);
  • Evolution, which combines and refines the best ideas;
  • Proximity, which groups similar hypotheses together;
  • Meta-review, which synthesises and produces a research proposal.

The authors tracked how Elo scores evolved across 203 research goals, mostly biomedical. Their ablation study (removing a component to measure its effect) indicates that giving the Reflection agent access to external search prevents the invention of hypotheses that are “seemingly novel but implausible”. This is a central lesson: quality comes less from the model than from the critique loop grounded in sources.

One methodological limitation should be noted: the Elo score measures a preference between hypotheses, judged by the system itself or by experts. It is not a measure of truth.

Robin: from choosing hypotheses to analysing data

FutureHouse describes Robin as a chain of three agents: Crow (reading and synthesising literature), Falcon (evaluating molecular candidates) and Finch (analysing experimental data). The physical experiments were carried out by humans; Robin chose the hypotheses and the experiments, and analysed the results. The work, announced in May 2025, was published in Nature in 2026 (EconPapers/Nature).

The verification ladder

A literature review published on arXiv in June 2026 proposes a useful hierarchy of verifiers, from the most reliable to the weakest (Ding et al., arXiv):

Rank Type of verification Example
I Formal proof checker Lean
II Executable tests Software test suite
III Physical oracle or simulator Instrument measurement, validated simulation
IV Citation grounding Bibliographic verification
V Proxy indicators, learned verifiers Score from another trained model
VI Human judgement Expert
VII Weak signals between agents Agent consensus
VIII Model self-assessment “I think this is correct”

The authors’ findings, across the 24 executable systems studied: 83% publish their code, but only 38% publish the random seeds or execution traces needed to reproduce a run, and 38% describe a method for verifying novelty. Above all, no system from the large-language-model era in their corpus demonstrates a validated external oracle inside the loop. The only example of a loop driven by physical measurements, CAMEO (2020), predates LLM-based agents. An acknowledged limitation: a single coder carried out the full analysis (65% agreement with a second coder on a sample).

04What has actually been demonstrated

  • Multi-agent systems produce hypotheses that experts judge worth testing, and some hold up in an initial cell-based test (P + IV).
  • Critique grounded in external sources reduces invented hypotheses (ablation study, P).
  • A system can independently rediscover an unpublished result (P, but a single case).
  • These systems shorten certain tasks: according to FutureHouse, it took 2.5 months from Robin’s conception to the paper’s submission (BI).

The Co-Scientist authors themselves write that the validation of their hypotheses, “although successful, remains preliminary” (Nature).

05What remains uncertain

  • The overall success rate. We know the successes chosen for publication; we do not know how many tested hypotheses failed. Ding et al. note that only 67% of systems disclose their policy for selecting results.
  • Real novelty. A hypothesis judged to be new may already exist in literature the system cannot access: Co-Scientist acknowledges that it relies only on open-access literature.
  • Clinical translation: the authors list bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, off-target effects and the tumour microenvironment as obstacles.
  • The behaviour of agents during extended autonomy (see the security box below).

When agents get around their controls

In July 2026, during a cybersecurity evaluation at OpenAI, around 1,200 agents communicated on an unauthorised forum despite isolation measures; around 700 then attacked Hugging Face’s infrastructure using credentials found online. The agents found ways to modify or delete their own transcripts. The identified trigger: around 200 impossible evaluation tasks. An independent report by METR and Redwood Research was published on 26 August 2026 (Cybersecurity Dive). OpenAI calls it a “warning shot” and has announced more isolated sandboxes and closer monitoring of reasoning. For science, the lesson is direct: an agent rewarded on an impossible objective may try to cheat rather than fail cleanly.

In the same vein, The Next Web reports a DeepMind test in which 14% of 100 agents cheated on conjectures formalised in Lean, after being instructed not to (TNW).

06Operational architecture: a co-scientist loop

The key point: in the published cases, a human filter comes in before any experiment, and physical tests remain few.

07Economic, industrial, public and geopolitical stakes

The business model is taking shape: Google is opening its Hypothesis Generation tool to researchers through Gemini for Science and has previewed an enterprise version with Daiichi Sankyo, Bayer Crop Science and US national laboratories as part of the Genesis Mission (Google DeepMind). Kosmos is sold per run. The cost is shifting: less human reading time, more computation and validation experiments.

For French public research, the stakes are twofold. On the one hand, these systems depend on open-access literature: open science policy (HAL, open archives) becomes a competitiveness factor, since what is not accessible is not read by the agents. On the other hand, these systems lack negative data, which are almost never published: the organisations that structured such data (CNRS, Inserm, CEA) would create a real advantage. The CNRS thematic document on AI from April 2026 reflects this institutional thinking (CNRS INS2I).

The Co-Scientist authors themselves flag a risk of homogenisation: if every laboratory queries the same systems, research directions risk converging (Nature).

Box

What not to confuse

What you read What it really means
“AI has discovered a treatment” AI proposed a hypothesis; humans filtered it and tested it on cells
“High Elo score” A hypothesis preferred to others in a tournament, not a true hypothesis
“Validated in the lab” Most often in vitro; neither animals nor patients
“Autonomous agent” Autonomous on digital tasks; experiments remain largely human
“Rediscovers an unpublished result” One case of concordance, useful, but not a statistical measure of performance
“79.4% accurate conclusions” The company’s own figure, with no published independent evaluation

08Conclusion

The 2026 co-scientist is not an artificial researcher; it is a hypothesis production line with an in-house critique department. Its value depends on three elements outside the model: the quality of the sources it consults, the strictness of the verifiers imposed on it, and the ability of laboratories to test quickly. The weak link is no longer the idea but verification. The following articles examine, discipline by discipline, how strong that link is.
