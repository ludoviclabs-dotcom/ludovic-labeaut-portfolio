Medicine, biology and drugs: accelerating discovery without confusing hypothesis, preclinical and clinical evidence

The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded in particular to the creators of AlphaFold, established AI as a tool of biology. Two years on, the announcements are multiplying: protein binders with “ten times the affinity”, agents proposing drugs against AMD or leukaemia, the first phase 3 trial of a molecule designed by generative AI. Yet in medicine, the distance between a hypothesis and a treatment is measured in years and in thousands of patients. As of the date of this article, no drug discovered by AI has been authorised by a regulatory agency, according to the sources consulted. This article puts every result back in its place on the scale of evidence.

By Ludovic Labeaut9 min readState of knowledge as of 8 October 2026

01The question

Is AI really speeding up the development of drugs, or only the upstream stages, the least costly and the least risky ones? And how can this acceleration be reconciled with two strong European constraints: the protection of health data and the prevention of biological dual use?

02The technology, explained

The scale of evidence in medicine

Step Question asked Typical duration AI example 2024–2026

In vitro Does it act on isolated cells or proteins? Weeks to months Binimetinib on leukaemia cell lines; ripasudil on retinal cells
Preclinical Does it act on organoids or animals, without prohibitive toxicity? 1 to 3 years Vorinostat on liver organoids
Phase 1 Is it tolerated in humans? About 1 year Molecules from “AI-native” companies
Phase 2 Does it show a signal of efficacy? 1 to 2 years Rentosertib, phase 2a
Phase 3 Is the efficacy confirmed on a large scale? 2 to 4 years Rentosertib, phase 3 launched in September 2026
Authorisation Is the benefit/risk balance favourable? About 1 year No documented case

Three families of tools

  1. Structure models: AlphaFold 3 predicts the shape of complexes between proteins, nucleic acids and small molecules; its open competitors are OpenFold3 and Boltz-2 (article 1).
  2. Sequence models: Evo 2 for genomes, ESM3 for proteins. ESM3, published in Science in 2025, was used to design a fluorescent protein far removed from known natural proteins (doi 10.1126/science.ads0018).
  3. Reasoning agents: Co-Scientist, Robin, Mythos, which combine literature, databases and tools (article 2).

03Documented use cases

AlphaFold: the benchmark, and its limits

In 2024 the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences honoured Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for protein structure prediction, and David Baker for the computational design of proteins (Nobel Prize). AlphaFold 3, published in Nature on 8 May 2024, extends prediction to complexes with ligands and nucleic acids. The authors list a number of limitations: chirality violations, “hallucinations” of ordered structures in disordered regions, prediction of static structures rather than dynamic ensembles. Most of the authors declare commercial interests. Code and weights were made available for academic research in November 2024 (Google).

The first phase 3 trial: rentosertib

The phase 2a trial published in Nature Medicine concerns rentosertib, from Insilico Medicine, a TNIK kinase inhibitor against idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, whose target and molecule were reportedly identified by AI. Method: randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 71 patients in 21 centres in China, 12 weeks. Primary endpoint: safety (the proportion of patients with at least one adverse event), not efficacy. Exploratory result: forced vital capacity improved by 98.4 ml in the 60 mg group, against a fall of 20.3 ml on placebo. 16 of the 71 patients stopped treatment, more of them in the high-dose groups; some discontinuations are linked to liver injury. Employees of the sponsor took part in the design, the analysis and the interpretation.

On 10 September 2026, Insilico announced the first patient dosed in the phase 3 GENESIS-IPF-3 trial: 320 participants planned in 47 centres in China, 52 weeks, with the annual decline in forced vital capacity as the primary endpoint (Insilico Medicine). The coordinator estimates that it will take three to four years “under favourable conditions” to reach an authorisation. The molecule is not authorised anywhere.

Isomorphic Labs: a slipping timetable

Demis Hassabis said in January 2026 at Davos that Alphabet’s subsidiary Isomorphic Labs “plans to have its first clinical trials by the end of 2026”, whereas a year earlier he had been targeting the end of 2025 (Reuters). The company’s public announcements up to 7 October 2026 make no mention of a patient dosed (Isomorphic Labs): information not confirmed to date.

Agents and repurposing

  • Co-Scientist (Google): binimetinib active at nanomolar concentrations on acute myeloid leukaemia cell lines; vorinostat active on fibrotic liver organoids (Nature). Level IV and PC.
  • Robin (FutureHouse): ripasudil, already prescribed for glaucoma, increases phagocytosis in cultured retinal epithelial cells, a lead for dry AMD (FutureHouse). Level IV.

Repurposing drugs that are already authorised reduces the toxicological risk, but not the need for efficacy trials.

A high binding affinity measured in vitro is a first step. It says nothing about specificity, stability, immunogenicity or therapeutic efficacy.

Evo 2: designing genomes

Evo 2, published in Nature in 2026, was trained on genomes from every domain of life. Of 285 bacteriophage genomes designed, 16 propagated and inhibited their target bacterium (Arc Institute). This is a remarkable proof of concept and a modest success rate, which illustrates the gap between generation and function.

04What has actually been demonstrated

  • AI structure prediction is an established scientific achievement (P, Nobel 2024).
  • Agents produce repurposing hypotheses that pass in vitro and organoid tests (P + IV / PC).
  • A molecule from an AI platform has shown acceptable tolerability and an exploratory signal of efficacy in phase 2a (CL, small sample, sponsor involved).
  • According to a BCG analysis of the pipelines of “AI-native” companies, molecules discovered by AI pass phase 1 in 80 to 90% of cases, but phase 2 in about 40% of cases, a rate close to historical averages, on a small sample (Drug Discovery Today, 2024).

Reading: AI appears to produce molecules with the right pharmacological properties; nothing yet proves that it is better at choosing the targets that work in patients.

05What remains uncertain

  • The real effect on total development time: Insilico claims 18 months up to candidate nomination, but most of the time is spent in the clinic.
  • How far protein design benchmarks generalise beyond the chosen targets.
  • Data bias: the biomedical literature over-represents positive results, and Co-Scientist acknowledges that it has no access to negative results.
  • The boundary between therapeutic use and malicious use (see below).

Biosecurity and dual use

Anthropic has evaluated Mythos 5’s ability to predict properties of adeno-associated virus capsids, the vectors used in gene therapy, noting that the same ability could help design dangerous viruses (Anthropic). This is why Fable blocks professional biology queries, and Mythos is open only to vetted professionals in the United States. Microsoft and its partners showed that open tools could reformulate toxins so as to get past DNA suppliers’ filters, then improved those filters (C&EN). Gigi Kwik Gronvall (Johns Hopkins) warns: “one day there will be a problem that cannot be solved by better screening or better technology”.

06Operational architecture: an AI-assisted drug discovery chain

Two architectural points matter for Europe. First, federated learning: Eli Lilly’s TuneLab programme gives biotechs access to models trained on about $1 billion worth of proprietary data, without each participant’s data leaving its own environment (NVIDIA). Second, the hosting of health data, discussed below.

07Economic, industrial, public and geopolitical stakes

French health data is a rare asset: the Système national des données de santé (SNDS) covers almost the whole population. Its use by researchers has been held back by the hosting of the Health Data Hub at Microsoft: because of US extraterritorial laws (Cloud Act, FISA), the CNIL has never authorised the transfer of the SNDS to the Hub. On 23 April 2026, the government chose Scaleway to host it; a copy of the SNDS could be transferred between late 2026 and early 2027 (Le Monde).

At European level, the regulation on the European Health Data Space (EHDS, Regulation 2025/327) entered into force on 26 March 2025 and progressively organises the secondary use of health data for research (European Commission). On the AI side, the AI Act rules for high-risk systems embedded in regulated products, such as medical devices, have been postponed to 2 August 2028 by the digital omnibus (European Parliament).

Inserm presents AI as a lever for exploiting health data (Inserm), but we have found no documented public programme combining frontier agents and SNDS data (information not confirmed).

The United Kingdom has chosen a quantified mission: “trial-ready” drugs in 100 days by 2030, with £8 million for the OpenBind consortium at the Diamond synchrotron, tasked with producing protein–ligand structure data (techUK). It is a target, not a result, but it illustrates a strategy: investing in high-quality experimental data rather than in the model.

Finally, the asymmetry of access weighs on European research: Anthropic’s best-performing model for protein design is not accessible outside the United States to date.

Box

What not to confuse

What you read What it really means
“AI discovers a drug” A hypothesis or a candidate molecule, at best tested on cells
“Validated in the lab” In vitro, unless animals or patients are explicitly mentioned
“First AI drug in phase 3” A trial under way; results unknown; no authorisation
“Efficacy in phase 2a” Safety as the primary endpoint; exploratory efficacy in 71 patients
“A binder with ten times the affinity” An in vitro affinity measurement on three targets; not a drug
“Repurposing” A molecule whose tolerability is known, with efficacy in the new indication still to be proven
“AlphaFold solves folding” Predicts reliable static structures; dynamics and disordered regions remain difficult

08Conclusion

AI has already transformed structural biology and is beginning to speed up the generation of hypotheses and the design of molecules. It has not yet demonstrated that it increases the probability that a drug works in patients. The coming years will be decisive: the results of rentosertib’s phase 3, Isomorphic’s first trials, and above all the phase 2 success rate of molecules derived from AI. For France, the challenge is to open its health data to research within a sovereign framework, at the very moment when the best biology models are reserved for US users.
